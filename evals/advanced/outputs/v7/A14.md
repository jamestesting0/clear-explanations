### Calibrated power and energy

The calibration is `P = 2V − 200`, where `V` is the reading in millivolts and `P` is power in watts. Applying the offset as part of the calibration gives:

| Time (s) | Reading (mV) | Calibrated power (W) |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| 0 | 600 | 1,000 |
| 30 | 1,100 | 2,000 |
| 90 | 600 | 1,000 |
| 120 | 100 | 0 |

The most recent sampled power remains constant until the next timestamp. Thus, each interval's energy is its power multiplied by its duration. The zero-watt reading at 120 seconds begins a new level outside the target interval; it contributes no energy to `[0,120)`. [A14-CALIBRATION] [A14-SAMPLES]

| Interval (s) | Duration (s) | Held power (W) | Energy (J) | Energy (Wh) |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| [0, 30) | 30 | 1,000 | 30,000 | 8⅓ |
| [30, 90) | 60 | 2,000 | 120,000 | 33⅓ |
| [90, 120) | 30 | 1,000 | 30,000 | 8⅓ |
| **Total** | **120** | — | **180,000** | **50** |

Using the supplied unit conversions:

`180,000 J ÷ 3,600 = 50 Wh`

`50 Wh ÷ 1,000 = 0.050 kWh.`

The time-weighted mean power is

`180,000 J ÷ 120 s = 1,500 W.`

Equivalently, the three powers receive weights `30/120, 60/120, 30/120`: the 2,000 W level lasts twice as long as either 1,000 W interval. [A14-SAMPLES]

### The spreadsheet has two independent errors

The unweighted mean of all four powers is

`(1,000 + 2,000 + 1,000 + 0)/4 = 1,000 W.`

Multiplying by 120 seconds gives 120,000 joules. Dividing by 1,000 gives **120 kilojoules**, not kilowatt-hours. Converting joules to kilowatt-hours requires division by `3,600 × 1,000 = 3,600,000`. [A14-SPREADSHEET] [A14-SAMPLES]

Correcting only that divisor gives `120,000/3,600,000 = 0.033333… kWh`. The result remains wrong because the unweighted average gives equal influence to unequally spaced samples and gives the endpoint reading positive weight despite its zero duration in the target interval. The correct time average is 1,500 W, not 1,000 W.

### What trapezoidal integration would calculate

Trapezoidal integration averages each interval's two endpoint powers:

| Interval (s) | Endpoint-average power (W) | Energy (J) |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| [0, 30) | 1,500 | 45,000 |
| [30, 90) | 1,500 | 90,000 |
| [90, 120) | 500 | 15,000 |
| **Total** | — | **150,000** |

That gives **41⅔ Wh = 0.041666… kWh**.

This calculation corresponds to power changing linearly between samples, including a gradual decline from 1,000 W to zero during the final interval. The supplied model instead holds power at 1,000 W throughout that interval and changes it at 120 seconds. Rectangles give the exact integral of the stipulated hold model; trapezoids calculate a different interpolated load. They are not automatically an accuracy improvement. ### Comparison with the register

The displayed increase is

`12.052 − 12.000 = 0.052 kWh.`

Each endpoint may be wrong by up to 0.001 kWh. With no assumptions about dependence or probability distributions, the conservative error bound for their difference is the **sum** of those bounds:

`±(0.001 + 0.001) = ±0.002 kWh.`

The possible true increase is therefore **[0.050, 0.054] kWh**. The analog total falls within this range. For example, true cumulative readings of 12.001 and 12.051 kWh would each satisfy the stated error bounds and give exactly the analog increase of 0.050 kWh. This example establishes compatibility, not that those were the actual readings. [A14-REGISTER]

The observed difference between methods is 0.002 kWh, or 2 Wh, equal to the conservative register error bound. That bound is not a 95% confidence interval, and the packet gives no probability for values within it.

In a real installation, an approximate calibration or hold model would introduce additional uncertainty into the analog result. One would need to assess calibration accuracy, actual power changes between samples, and alignment of the measurement windows before judging agreement or replacing an instrument. Exact arithmetic establishes what follows **if the model holds**; it does not independently establish that the model represents the physical load.
