**The randomized comparison remains informative, but it concerns assignment within teams where exactly half the employees are invited.** It does not identify the effect of training everyone rather than nobody, because those two team policies were never randomized in this experiment. [B28-S1]

## What was observed and randomized

Assigned employees made (160/2{,}000=8\%) errors; unassigned employees made (200/2{,}000=10\%\). The observed difference is **−2 percentage points**, equivalent to a 20% lower error rate relative to the unassigned group's 10%. It is an estimate from this assignment, not an established exact effect for every employee or future rollout. The report supplies no uncertainty estimate. Employees were randomized; the 4,000 checks were not 4,000 independently randomized people. [B28-S1]

Within each team, the lottery selected exactly five of ten employees. Consequently:

| Employee's own assignment | Other assigned teammates |
| --- | ---: |
| Assigned | 4 |
| Unassigned | 5 |

The comparison therefore combines a difference in one's own invitation with a difference in peers' assignments. Randomization supports estimating the contrast between these two situations, averaged over the teammate identities that the lottery can produce. It does **not** isolate a change in one's own invitation while keeping every peer's assignment fixed: switching only that invitation would violate the fixed-five rule. If particular colleagues influence learning differently, averaging over their identities matters as well as counting them. [B28-S1, B28-S2, B28-S4]

## Assignment is not the same as exposure

An unassigned employee is someone who was not selected for the training, not necessarily someone who never encountered the checklist. Chat records document explanations reaching at least six unassigned employees. They do not establish everyone's exposure, the amount learned, or equivalence between receiving an explanation and receiving the training. [B28-S2]

Sharing is a **spillover**: another employee's assignment changes one's own outcome. It could convey useful knowledge, but training can also reduce colleagues' available helping time. The measured contrast includes the consequences of working within that allocation system. It does not separate the effect of one's own training from all these peer effects. Simply regrouping people by actual checklist exposure would not preserve the original randomized comparison, because exposure through colleagues was not itself randomly assigned. [B28-S1, B28-S2]

There is no established direction for the resulting difference from a pure own-training effect. Helpful sharing could reduce the assigned–unassigned gap, while time competition, different colleague identities, and other responses could change it differently. Thus “sharing must make the estimate too small” is unsupported. Sharing changes what the comparison answers; it does not make the lottery meaningless. [B28-S2, B28-S4]

## Why the other comparisons do not supply the missing answer

The historical 20% rate and current assigned-group 8% rate differ by 12 percentage points, but the assessments used different task versions. Time, task difficulty, and the different assignment and exposure conditions are mixed together. That subtraction cannot isolate training's effect. [B28-S3]

The distant teams' decline from 18% to 16% is also a two-point change, but those teams were not randomized alongside the experimental teams and had different tasks and experience. Their trend does not establish what the experimental teams would have done under universal training or no training. Nor is there evidence that their trend can simply be subtracted to correct the historical comparison. [B28-S3]

A company-wide rollout would change the entire allocation: under full training, each employee has nine assigned teammates; under none, each has zero. Neither situation occurred in the randomized teams. The current two-point gap therefore cannot be carried over as an exact universal-versus-none effect. [B28-S1, B28-S2, B28-S3]

## What a better design would answer

Randomly assigning many teams to **zero, partial, or full training** would address distinct questions:

- Comparing full-training and zero-training teams would estimate the total effect of those team policies, including their within-team sharing and time costs. This directly addresses rollout, provided important influences across teams are prevented or incorporated into the design.
- Comparing unassigned employees in partial-training teams with employees in zero-training teams would estimate effects on unassigned people of having trained colleagues. That measures the net spillover from the allocation, not necessarily a purely beneficial information effect.
- Randomizing individuals within partial-training teams remains useful for assignment contrasts. However, if those teams still receive exactly five invitations, the four-versus-five peer difference remains. A direct effect holding peer assignments fixed needs a design permitting one's own invitation to vary without forcing a peer change—for example, independently randomized individual invitations in suitable study arms. [B28-S2, B28-S4]

The analysis should use the team and individual randomization structure to quantify uncertainty. Forbidding all communication would instead test training under isolation. It may help study that restricted intervention, but it changes the workplace conditions whose sharing and coordination effects the actual rollout must accommodate. [B28-S4]
