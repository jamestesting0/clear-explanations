**The supplied numbers yield a descriptive difference-in-differences of 12 points, and one particular pretrend adjustment yields 6 points. Neither identifies the tool's causal effect.** There is also a data-validity problem to resolve first: North's week-1 value is **104%**, which cannot be the percentage of tickets resolved within two days. The calculations below treat the entries as supplied numbers; they must not be published as validated ticket-resolution percentages without correcting or explaining that entry. [A23-SERIES]

## Specified before-and-after calculation

The requested means give each week equal weight, irrespective of its ticket count. [A23-ANALYSIS]

| Center | Before: mean of weeks −2 and −1 | After: mean of weeks 0 and 1 | After minus before |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| North | `(84 + 88) / 2 = 86` | `(100 + 104) / 2 = 102` | `102 − 86 = 16` |
| South | `(51 + 52) / 2 = 51.5` | `(55 + 56) / 2 = 55.5` | `55.5 − 51.5 = 4` |

Thus the descriptive difference-in-differences is:

`North's change − South's change = 16 − 4 = 12 points`.

Equivalently, the North–South gap rises from `86 − 51.5 = 34.5` before to `102 − 55.5 = 46.5` after, an increase of 12. These are percentage-point calculations under the stated metric, not relative percentage changes. The North after-mean of 102 further exposes the invalid percentage entry. [A23-SERIES]

The presentation's 16 and 12 therefore match the formal arithmetic. What does not follow is its claim that either number measures the tool's effect.

## The missing causal comparison

The tool's effect compares what happened in North with what would have happened there over the same period **without the tool**. That second outcome is unobserved; it is the counterfactual.

To interpret the 12-point contrast as the tool's effect, the comparison must support the assumption that, without the tool, North's before-to-after change would have equaled South's four-point change. This is the relevant **parallel-trends assumption**: equality of changes in the absence of the intervention, not equality of outcome levels.

A stable level difference between centers would cancel when taking their changes. For example, North could consistently resolve a higher share than South while both improve by the same amount over time. Conversely, similar starting levels would not establish that they would subsequently follow the same trend.

Here, the observed pre-period pattern gives a concrete reason to question the assumption:

| Pre-period transition | North's change | South's change | Increase in North–South gap |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Week −3 to −2 | +4 | +1 | +3 |
| Week −2 to −1 | +4 | +1 | +3 |

The gap is already growing: 30, 33, then 36 points. A calculation that assigns all subsequent widening to the tool would ignore that earlier pattern. However, three pre-period observations do not prove how the untreated gap would have evolved after week zero. Trends could change, and percentages also have a ceiling. The pattern weakens the justification for a constant untreated gap; it does not reveal the missing counterfactual with certainty. [A23-SERIES; A23-CONTEXT]

## What the six-point calculation assumes

The average time in the before period is week `−1.5`; in the after period it is week `0.5`. Their separation is **two weeks**. If the untreated North–South gap literally kept widening by three points per week, its expected increase between these periods would be:

`3 points/week × 2 weeks = 6 points`.

Subtracting that modeled increase from the observed contrast gives:

`12 − 6 = 6 points`.

The same result follows by extending each center's pretrend separately. North's projected values at weeks 0 and 1 would be 92 and 96, averaging 94; its supplied after-mean of 102 exceeds that projection by 8. South's projected values would be 53 and 54, averaging 53.5; its after-mean of 55.5 exceeds that projection by 2. The difference between these departures is `8 − 2 = 6`. [Calculation from A23-SERIES]

This is a coherent **model-based descriptive adjustment**. It adds an assumption that the previous trend difference would have persisted. It does not establish that assumption, nor show that any remaining North-specific change came from the tool. Even perfectly fitted pre-period lines cannot identify which cause produced a post-period departure.

## Staffing, case mix, and the pretrend test

North received five experienced temporary staff at the same time it adopted the tool. A staffing benefit would appear in the North-specific post-rollout change alongside any tool benefit. The tool and staffing might also work differently together than separately. With both introduced in one center at the same time, this packet cannot separate their contributions. Subtracting a historical trend does not remove a new staffing effect. [A23-CONTEXT]

Uninvestigated changes in the types of tickets or customers could also change resolution percentages. A consistent resolution definition helps make measurements comparable, but does not establish that the work arriving at each center remained comparable. Management's nonrandom choices about both interventions add to the difficulty of interpreting the contrast causally. [A23-CONTEXT]

The reported nonsignificant pretrend test does not prove parallel trends. Failure to detect a trend difference can reflect limited precision or other features of the test; without its model, sample size, or uncertainty, its power cannot be assessed. Nor could a test of past trends establish future untreated trends by itself. The visible numerical pattern should be discussed directly, without inventing a p-value, standard error, or confidence interval. [A23-CLAIMS; A23-ANALYSIS]

## A feasible follow-up

Use the available ability to **randomize tool rollout timing across additional comparable centers**, comparing early-rollout centers with centers still waiting during the same calendar periods. For a tool-only estimate, hold staffing at its usual level in both groups during that comparison. This identifies the tool's contribution under usual staffing, without requiring North's completed rollout to be reversed. [A23-DECISION]

The strongest extension for deciding among tool, staffing, and both is to randomize the two interventions independently, if staffing assignment can also be randomized:

| Group | Tool | Additional staffing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | No, during the comparison period | No |
| 2 | Yes | No |
| 3 | No, during the comparison period | Yes |
| 4 | Yes | Yes |

Comparing group 2 with group 1 estimates the tool-only effect; group 3 with group 1 estimates staffing alone; and group 4 with group 1 estimates the combined package. Comparing group 4 with group 3 measures the added contribution of the tool when extra staff are already present.

Only randomized tool timing is explicitly available in the packet. If staffing cannot also be randomized, randomize tool timing separately within centers with usual staffing and centers with planned extra staffing, keeping staffing stable within each comparison. That can estimate the tool's incremental effect in each staffing setting. It does **not** automatically make differences between the staffing settings causal.

Measure staffing exposure, case mix, ticket counts, and ticket-level outcomes; prespecify the comparison periods and weighting. Analyze uncertainty in a way that respects assignment at the center level, since tickets within one center share its treatment and working conditions. Track actual adoption and any sharing of staff or work across groups that could blur the comparison.

**What can be reported now:** as entered, the table's specified arithmetic produces changes of 16 and 4 points, a 12-point descriptive contrast, and a 6-point contrast under an explicit linear-trend extrapolation. The 104% entry needs correction before these become credible rate summaries, and any correction requires recalculation. Even with valid rates, the supplied design would not distinguish a tool-only causal effect from staffing, trend changes, and changing case mix. [A23-SERIES; A23-CONTEXT]
