**Both descriptions of the accepted-request results are arithmetically correct: success increased within Easy and Hard requests, while the overall success rate fell.** The tool period accepted a much larger proportion of Hard requests. That change in composition can outweigh improvements within both groups. These comparisons do not establish the tool’s causal effect or district fairness.

## The overall rate changes with the mix

Using the supplied counts:

| Accepted-request group | Baseline successes / accepted | Baseline rate | Tool-period successes / accepted | Tool-period rate |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Easy | 90 / 100 | 90% | 19 / 20 | 95% |
| Hard | 20 / 100 | 20% | 60 / 200 | 30% |
| All accepted requests | 110 / 200 | **55%** | 79 / 220 | **35.91%** |

The within-group increases are **5 percentage points** for Easy requests and **10 percentage points** for Hard requests. The overall decline is approximately **19.09 percentage points**. [Calculated from B10-S1]

An overall rate is an average of group rates weighted by how many accepted requests belong to each group. In the baseline, half were Hard. In the tool period, 200 / 220, or **90.91%**, were Hard. Consequently:

- Baseline: 0.5 × 90% + 0.5 × 20% = **55%**.
- Tool period: (20 / 220) × 95% + (200 / 220) × 30% = **35.91%**.

The later period gives much more weight to the group whose success rate remains lower. There is no arithmetic contradiction. The claim that the overall rate “must” rise ignores those changing weights. The pooled decline also cannot show that every type of customer was harmed; the observed rates rose within both supplied groups. [B10-S1, B10-S3]

## What the reweighted result means

To compare category rates using the baseline’s equal category mix, give each tool-period rate a weight of one half:

**0.5 × 95% + 0.5 × 30% = 62.5%.**

This is **7.5 percentage points above** the baseline’s 55%. It answers: *What average would the observed tool-period category rates produce if Easy and Hard each received half the weight?* This procedure, called standardization, holds the reported category mix fixed for the comparison. It does not change the actual tool-period result of 79 timely completions among 220 accepted requests. [Calculated from B10-S1]

Calling the 7.5-point difference the “causal benefit of the tool” goes beyond the evidence. Causal attribution asks what would have happened to comparable requests under the alternative of not using the tool. Reweighting these two categories does not supply that missing comparison.

The contract changed, training changed, managers made undocumented overrides, and the acceptance process changed. Even within Hard requests, the distribution of unrecorded severity may differ. The fixed category definition rules out a change in the label’s definition, but not changes in who enters each category’s accepted sample. Without a suitable comparison group or stronger evidence, neither the pooled decline nor the standardized increase isolates the tool’s contribution. [B10-S2]

## What “customer outcomes” and “fairness” still require

The reported proportion measures one outcome among accepted requests: completion within seven days. All those outcomes are observed, so missing follow-up among accepted requests does not explain this comparison. But a later satisfactory repair still counts as a failure for this metric. [B10-S1]

The reported samples contain **110 timely completions before and 79 after**. Those counts do not measure eventual successful repairs, waiting before acceptance, access to the promise or the number turned away. Without comparable period lengths, they also should not be presented as a change in completions per unit time. The missing rejected and abandoned requests prevent evaluating the service received by the full incoming population. Their absence does not establish how large unmet demand was. [B10-S1–S2, B10-S4]

District fairness is likewise unresolved. The packet supplies no East–West cross-tabulation, and the policy does not define which differences make requests comparable. Equal completion rates among accepted requests could coexist with unequal access—for example, if one district had to clear a more restrictive selection process. That is a possible mechanism, not a finding about either district. [B10-S4]

## A defensible evaluation

First, define the policy comparison with district representatives and the customer council. Agree which recorded factors legitimately justify different access—such as repair difficulty, more detailed severity, urgency and required resources—and specify the time origin for each waiting-time and completion measure. Define an acceptable access gap in advance rather than declaring fairness after seeing the results.

Then collect a record for **every incoming request**, including district, those comparison factors, recommendation, actual acceptance decision, override reason, waiting time, rejection or abandonment, and eventual repair outcome. Report both all incoming requests and any explicitly defined eligible subset, so eligibility decisions remain visible.

For East and West, compare acceptance proportions and waiting times **within the agreed comparison groups**, then calculate district summaries using a common request mix. Report group sizes and uncertainty alongside the differences. Separately compare seven-day completion among accepted requests, timely repairs per incoming request and eventual successful repairs. Together these show whether similar requests gain similar access and what happens after access is granted.

Finally, test the tool against a concurrent comparison under the same contract and training conditions. If feasible, randomly assign comparable operational units or requests to tool-assisted decisions versus the established process, with a predefined policy for overrides and follow-up of all incoming requests. This would make it possible to attribute differences more credibly to the tool while examining access, completion and district disparities together. If randomization is infeasible, a prospective concurrent comparison is still useful, but remaining differences between the groups must remain part of the causal uncertainty.
