**With the existing workshop, the best weekly mix is 100 Standard repairs and 10 Rush repairs, producing profit of 6,300 currency units.** Rush contributes more per job, but Standard contributes more per workshop hour. Buying the additional 30-hour block raises the best achievable profit to 7,050, a gain of 750 under the supplied demand assumptions. [B13-S1, B13-S3; calculations below]

## What the proposed schedule changes

Revenue is sales income. Contribution is revenue minus variable materials costs; it pays the fixed expenses and then provides profit. Here, profit equals contribution minus the existing weekly fixed expenses of 2,000. Those fixed expenses do not change with the mix. [B13-S1]

| Weekly measure | Baseline: 100 Standard, 0 Rush | Proposal: 80 Standard, 20 Rush | Change |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Workshop hours used | 100 | 120 | +20 |
| Revenue | 10,000 | 11,600 | +1,600 |
| Variable materials costs | 3,000 | 3,400 | +400 |
| Contribution | 7,000 | 8,200 | +1,200 |
| Fixed expenses | 2,000 | 2,000 | 0 |
| Profit | 5,000 | 6,200 | +1,200 |

These are calculated from the prices, costs, and schedules in [B13-S1–S2]. For example, proposed revenue is 80 × 100 + 20 × 180 = 11,600; proposed materials cost is 80 × 30 + 20 × 50 = 3,400.

The revenue increase is therefore **1,600, or 16% of baseline revenue**. The profit increase is **1,200, or 24% of baseline profit**. The amounts differ because materials cost rises by 400; the relative percentages also use different baselines. Contribution rises by about 17.1%, from 7,000 to 8,200. Revenue growth and profit growth are not interchangeable.

## Why Standard gets priority, but cannot fill the week

| Product | Contribution per job | Hours per job | Contribution per workshop hour |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Standard | 100 − 30 = 70 | 1 | 70 |
| Rush | 180 − 50 = 130 | 2 | 65 |

One Rush job occupies time that could accommodate two Standard jobs. Where demand permits either choice, those two Standard jobs contribute 140, compared with 130 from Rush. Giving that time to Rush sacrifices 10 of contribution. The already-committed fixed expenses do not change this comparison. [B13-S1]

However, the shop can sell at most 100 Standard repairs. It should therefore perform those 100 jobs, using 100 hours, and use the remaining 20 hours for 10 Rush jobs. Both demand ceilings are respected, and all jobs are whole jobs. [B13-S1]

The resulting contribution is:

**100 × 70 + 10 × 130 = 8,300**, giving profit of **8,300 − 2,000 = 6,300**.

This mix is best because it uses all 120 hours while allocating as many hours as demand allows to the higher-contribution-per-hour product. Replacing any two available Standard jobs with one Rush job would reduce contribution.

The proposed 80/20 schedule earns 100 less: relative to the best mix, it replaces 20 Standard jobs with 10 Rush jobs, losing 1,400 and gaining 1,300 in contribution. Following the manager’s “Rush first” priority would instead accept the maximum 30 Rush jobs and then 60 Standard jobs, earning profit of 6,100. Filling all 120 hours with Rush alone would require 60 Rush jobs, twice the stated demand ceiling. [B13-S1–S2; derived]

## Evaluate extra capacity against the best existing mix

With 150 hours, the best mix becomes **100 Standard and 25 Rush**. It uses all 150 hours and stays below the Rush demand ceiling of 30. Compared with the best existing mix, the extra block enables 15 additional Rush jobs. [B13-S1, B13-S3]

| Increment from buying the block | Currency units per week |
| --- | ---: |
| Additional revenue: 15 × 180 | +2,700 |
| Additional materials: 15 × 50 | −750 |
| Additional contribution | +1,950 |
| Full block fee | −1,200 |
| Additional profit | **+750** |

As a check, total profit becomes 100 × 70 + 25 × 130 − 2,000 − 1,200 = **7,050**.

The offer improves profit in this model because additional contribution exceeds the full fee. Extra revenue alone would not establish that. With fewer than 10 additional Rush jobs, their contribution would not cover the 1,200 fee; demand sufficient to use profitable capacity matters.

## What these results establish

The calculations identify the best weekly mix and capacity choice **at the supplied prices, costs, and exact demand ceilings**. They do not identify better prices: the packet provides no evidence about how demand would respond to a price change. Nor does a short reservation survey establish future paid demand outside the exercise; cancellations and actual willingness to purchase remain to be checked. [B13-S4]

Shop profit also does not measure customer welfare or overall social benefit. Higher Rush prices do not reveal customers’ benefit above the price they pay, and the packet supplies neither those values nor effects on other shops. “Socially optimal” is therefore unsupported. [B13-S4]

Within the model, adding 10 Rush jobs to the baseline’s idle hours raises profit by 1,300 without reducing Standard work. That is a conditional accounting comparison. The observed baseline and proposed schedules do not, by themselves, establish the real-world causal effect of introducing Rush: actual uptake, changes over time, or effects on Standard demand would require evidence beyond the model’s assumptions.
