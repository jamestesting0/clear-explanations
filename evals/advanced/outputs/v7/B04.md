The trial recorded **70 fewer expired items and 700 currency units more contribution before unchanged expenses**. It did not establish that forecasting improved by 50% or that customer service was preserved. North’s stockout time increased sharply, while South gained a new customer and received more stock. Those changes make the aggregate result insufficient grounds for immediate expansion.

These calculations use the supplied ledger and availability logs. Total stockout hours are summed across stores, not elapsed hours for a single store. [B04-S1–S2]

Waste fell by `(140 − 70) / 140 = 50%`. The share of receipts discarded fell from 10% to 5%: a decrease of 5 percentage points, also a 50% relative reduction. Neither quantity measures forecast error. The packet supplies neither forecasts nor a comparison between forecasts and actual demand from which a forecast-accuracy improvement could be calculated. [B04-S1, B04-S4]

Sales divided by receipts measures **sell-through: how much received stock was sold**. It does not measure the proportion of customers whose needs were met. Ordering very little can produce excellent sell-through while leaving many customers unable to buy. With zero opening and closing stock and no transfers, receipts equal sales plus expired items in every row. Because total receipts stayed at 1,400, the 70-item reduction in waste and 70-item increase in sales are two sides of the same accounting identity, not independent evidence of better service. [B04-S1]

## Contribution increased, but unevenly

Under the supplied accounting assumptions:

`contribution before unchanged expenses = 10 × items sold − 6 × items received`

Acquisition costs include expired stock, because the distributor paid for those items too.

| Measure, currency units | Baseline | Trial | Change |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Sales revenue | 12,600 | 13,300 | +700 |
| Item acquisition cost | 8,400 | 8,400 | 0 |
| Contribution before unchanged expenses | 4,200 | 4,900 | **+700** |
| North’s contribution | 3,000 | 2,800 | −200 |
| South’s contribution | 1,200 | 2,100 | +900 |

An independent reconciliation is `4 × items sold − 6 × expired items`: baseline `4 × 1,260 − 6 × 140 = 4,200`; trial `4 × 1,330 − 6 × 70 = 4,900`. This gives the same totals. [B04-S1]

Because disposal and other expenses are stated to be unchanged, they do not alter the 700-unit change. Their missing amounts prevent calculation of the absolute result after those expenses. Most importantly, the observed increase is not yet an estimate of how much the forecasting system caused.

## Several causal stories fit these observations

**North may have reduced excess stock while also ordering too little at important times.** Receipts fell by 200 items, sales by 140, and waste by 60. Time without stock rose from 2 to 18 of 200 opening hours: from 1% to 9%. That documents poorer stock availability by this measure, although it does not count disappointed customers. Stockouts depend on supply quantities and timing as well as purchasing demand, so the increase does not prove total demand rose. Demand could have fallen after the bus-stop closure while supply fell further or arrived at less useful times. The two queue notes suggest possible periods of concentrated demand, but do not quantify a period-wide change. [B04-S1–S3]

**South may have matched stock better, benefited from its new customer, or both.** Receipts increased by 200 items, sales by 210, and waste fell by 10, with stockout time unchanged. The cafeteria purchased 120 items. Subtracting those identified purchases leaves 450 trial sales, compared with 360 baseline sales. Across both stores, trial sales excluding the cafeteria were 1,210, versus 1,260 at baseline. These are descriptive decompositions, not estimates of what would have happened without the cafeteria or without the system: the contract could also have affected ordering and other customers’ purchases. [B04-S1–S3]

The distributor received 200 fewer items at North and 200 more at South; this was a change in the distribution of incoming stock, not a transfer between stores. A new large customer could make that allocation beneficial even without improved forecasting. The system might also have helped make it. The packet does not distinguish those explanations. [B04-S1, B04-S3]

Both stores adopted the system simultaneously, with no comparison stores. Road construction, the cafeteria contract, and other period changes could affect results. Selecting stores for unusually high baseline waste also makes a subsequent fall easier to observe even without a useful intervention: an unusually bad period may not repeat. The marketing statement adds no evidence about these stores. [B04-S2–S3]

## Sales do not reveal all demand

Observed sales are completed purchases. Demand also includes purchases people wanted to make but could not complete under the available stock and service conditions. The counter misses departures, intended purchases replaced by substitutes, and people deterred by the empty display. Historical sales can therefore understate demand when stock is unavailable; a system trained on those sales may learn the effects of prior shortages instead of the demand it needs to meet. [B04-S2–S3]

These records cannot identify a numerical total of unmet demand. Stockout hours provide neither customer arrival counts nor desired quantities during those hours. They establish no positive numerical lower bound on lost units and no finite numerical upper bound. Applying an average observed sales rate to stockout hours would require unsupported assumptions about when customers arrived, what they wanted, and whether they deferred or substituted purchases. The queue notes do not supply those missing counts.

## A feasible next evaluation

Expand as a controlled evaluation across stores with the customer patterns relevant to the eventual rollout. Collect several baseline periods, group comparable stores by traffic and contract mix, then randomly assign stores within those groups to use the system now or continue the existing ordering process during the same weeks. Track bus access, new contracts, promotions, and other changes rather than assuming they balance in a small sample.

Record forecasts, actual orders, delivery times, inventory, expiry, sales, and the timing of stockouts. Add systematic recording of requests that cannot be filled and short customer observations or surveys; acknowledge that purchases abandoned before entering remain harder to measure. Assess waste and contribution alongside availability and successful fulfillment of recorded requests. This would expose a policy that reduces expiry mainly by starving shelves, and show whether benefits persist beyond stores receiving a new large customer.

Even a successful reconstruction of demand would solve a measurement problem, not automatically the causal problem. Knowing demand would help assess matching and shortages, but the present before-and-after record would still lack the result those stores would have achieved without the system under the same conditions. A contemporaneous randomized comparison supplies evidence for that missing comparison.
