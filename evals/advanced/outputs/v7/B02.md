The reports answer different questions, so their results need not conflict. Together they suggest that Luma can reduce heat entering a roof under some conditions, but they do **not establish that it improves residents’ comfort in the town’s actual housing**. The strongest direct comparison of occupied buildings is uncertain, and none of the reports closely matches the town’s main problem: discomfort in top-floor apartments during humid evenings.

## What was assigned or selected, and what was measured?

| Report | How the comparison arose | What it measured | Claim it can support |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Randomized pilot | Twenty buildings were assigned to one coating or the other | Average afternoon indoor temperature over eight weeks; a comfort questionnaire with incomplete responses | An uncertain estimate of Luma’s average temperature effect in the studied buildings |
| Contractor comparison | Owners chose coating and survey participation | Reported afternoons of discomfort | An association between being in the coated group and reporting less discomfort |
| Chamber study | Paired roof panels were tested under controlled conditions | Inward heat flow | A physical effect under the tested panel and chamber conditions |

These distinctions determine how far each result can be carried. [B02-S1–S3]

### The pilot: a useful design with an imprecise result

Random assignment makes the pilot better able to separate the coating’s effect from pre-existing building differences. However, the coating was assigned to **20 buildings**, not independently to 740 apartments. Apartments within a building share ventilation and roof exposure, so their measurements are partly linked. Treating them as independent exaggerates how much independent evidence the study contains. The later, narrower interval does not settle the question by turning apartments into additional independent assignments. The building-adjusted analysis is the relevant one here. [B02-S1]

Its estimated temperature difference is −0.6 units, with an uncertainty interval from −1.4 to +0.2. Negative values mean cooler treated buildings. Thus the estimate favors cooling, but the interval also includes no average difference and a small temperature increase. Crossing zero does not prove no effect; it means this analysis cannot distinguish those possibilities with the reported precision. Calling the study “underpowered” describes a concern about its ability to detect an effect, not evidence that the true effect is either zero or beneficial. [B02-S1–S2]

The comfort questionnaire presents a separate problem. Response was 78% in controls and 51% in treated buildings, and reasons for nonresponse are unknown. People who responded may differ from those who did not, potentially in different ways between groups. Residents also knew their coating assignment, which could affect reported comfort. These facts do not tell us the direction of any bias, but random assignment alone does not repair missing questionnaire responses. The packet supplies no comfort results from which to estimate that outcome. [B02-S1]

### The contractor: more observations do not remove other explanations

Coated buildings reported 30% fewer afternoons of discomfort, but 70% also had external shades, compared with 15% of uncoated buildings. Shades could explain part of the difference. Insulation, neighborhood, prior discomfort, and the processes by which owners chose coating and survey participation could also matter. Adjusting for building age does not address those differences. [B02-S2]

The 30% figure is therefore a comparison between selected groups, not an established effect of Luma alone. More buildings can make a systematically distorted comparison look precise without making it causal. Missing raw comfort scores and recruitment records limit further checking. Nor can the packet translate the relative 30% reduction into an absolute number of avoided uncomfortable afternoons without the underlying baseline. [B02-S2]

### The chamber: evidence for a physical mechanism, with a narrow setting

The 18% reduction in inward heat flow supports the proposed physical benefit in the six paired panels under fixed radiation, equal airflow, dry surfaces, and no internal heat sources. It is not an 18% reduction in room temperature or discomfort. Occupied homes add other sources of heat and different ventilation, weather, and roof materials. The tested substrate matches only one of the town’s three common roof materials. [B02-S3]

The wet-surface estimate of 3% raises a useful question about conditions affecting performance, but absent replicate counts and uncertainty, it cannot establish a precise wet-versus-dry difference. A humid evening is also not automatically equivalent to the chamber’s wet-surface experiment. [B02-S3–S4]

Manufacturer funding warrants attention to methods and reporting; it neither invalidates nor validates the measurements. The available methods and full panel results permit scrutiny. The design award supplies no evidence about heat or comfort. [B02-S3–S4]

## What should the committee conclude and study next?

A benefit remains plausible, but its size and relevance locally remain unresolved. Half the pilot buildings were in a drier area outside town, and afternoon temperature does not directly answer a question about humid-evening comfort. The chamber cannot remove the contractor study’s competing explanations merely by showing that a physical mechanism is possible. [B02-S1–S4]

A small average benefit could still matter greatly in the hottest apartments, or could conceal little benefit there. An average cannot distinguish those patterns, and this packet provides no suitable subgroup results. [B02-S1, B02-S4]

The most valuable next study would be a **local randomized comparison at building level**, designed around humid-evening comfort and top-floor apartments. It should:

- Include enough independently assigned buildings across the three common roof materials, with coating groups balanced on baseline conditions and existing shades and insulation.
- Prespecify a comfort measure, collect it consistently with active follow-up of nonresponders, and record why responses are missing. Pair it with indoor temperature and humidity measurements during the relevant hours.
- Record concurrent building changes and predefine comparisons for the hottest apartments and roof materials. Because buildings differ greatly in size, specify whether the overall result represents an average building or an average resident.
- Analyze the shared building assignment correctly and plan precision around a benefit residents would consider worthwhile.

This would address three specific gaps together: whether Luma causes a difference, whether that difference concerns comfort, and whether it occurs in the housing and conditions the town cares about. Obtaining the pilot’s building-level results, local subgroup data, questionnaire results, and nonresponse information would help plan it, but simply adding more apartments to the existing analysis would not resolve those gaps.
