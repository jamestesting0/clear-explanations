The passage makes suspicion of Mara possible while repeatedly exposing the narrator’s desire to turn suspicion into proof. Its strongest invitation is to read the account as an unstable attempt at self-exoneration, complicated by later admissions of shared responsibility. That reading does not settle who physically opened the gate, or establish that Mara was innocent.

## A verdict arrives before the evidence

“I remember the gate opening because I remember being innocent” makes innocence the foundation of memory. Ordinarily, an account of what happened would support a judgment about responsibility; here, the narrator begins with the desired judgment and uses it to authorize the account. The next sentence immediately weakens the apparent certainty: the bell had not sounded, “unless” the sound was a preliminary test. The narrator is confident about innocence while uncertain about the sequence that could help establish it. [B09-S1]

The missing boat repeats this pattern. Its absence becomes “proof” of the brother’s escape, although it establishes neither escape nor death. The retrospective qualification—“in those days I called many absences proof”—shows that the older narrator recognizes the younger narrator’s faulty reasoning. This recognition gives the account some critical distance without making every later statement reliable. A person can acknowledge one evasion while continuing another. [B09-S1]

The shifts between the remembered scene and the later voice therefore matter morally. We hear both someone defending a past self and someone exposing how that defense worked.

## Objects invite accusations they cannot sustain

The key appears initially to locate responsibility with Mara: “You have the key.” Her answer redirects attention to the narrator’s empty belt and repeated reaching. That gesture could suggest habit, earlier possession or anxious concern. It does not establish who currently has the key, much less who operated the gate. Mara’s reply makes the accuser part of the scene’s uncertainty. [B09-S1]

Her hidden hands similarly withhold information before the red palm seems to reveal it. But the passage immediately supplies an alternative explanation: every public handle has been painted. Redness cannot uniquely connect her hand to this wheel. The sentence “I wanted the color to be an accusation” makes the inferential pressure explicit: the narrator wants an ambiguous mark to deliver a verdict. The classroom handout’s universal “code for guilt” overlooks the competing explanation deliberately present in this text. [B09-S2, B09-S4]

Other objects question what it means to make something visible. Water “flat as a letter no one had opened” suggests meaning or danger that remains unread. The keeper’s lantern offers the familiar appearance of illumination but adds no actual light in daylight. It can suggest the gap between possessing an instrument of inquiry and discovering anything through it. The margin instruction to keep the lantern unnecessary supports the significance of that uselessness in the drafting process; it does not establish a single obligatory symbolism. [B09-S1–S2, B09-S4]

## Three different kinds of responsibility

The keeper asks who **requested** the opening. The narrator says “She did”; Mara says “We did.” These statements compete over the scope of participation: the narrator singles her out, while Mara includes someone else, most naturally the narrator. “We” challenges the isolation of blame without identifying whose hands turned the wheel. Both answers also reach us through the narrator’s later account, rather than an independent transcript. [B09-S2]

The keeper declines to record either answer and asks about warning the lower houses. His question shifts attention from authorization to the protection of people exposed to the consequences. The passage thus separates three questions:

- **Physical action:** Who operated the gate?
- **Authorization:** Who requested, agreed to or supported opening it?
- **Warning:** Who knew of the danger and had a responsibility and opportunity to alert the houses?

These need not have the same answer. Someone could share responsibility without turning the wheel; someone who did turn it might not be solely responsible for the decision or its consequences.

The narrator’s preference for looking at Mara’s hand instead of the path toward the houses is particularly revealing. Accusing her is “easier” than attending to the people below. That supports a reading of avoidance. It does not establish whether warnings were actually omitted, whether the narrator could have delivered them in time, or what Mara did about them. [B09-S2, B09-S4]

## What the clearer notice leaves behind

“No one opens this gate alone” can be read as a practical rule requiring company. It also permits a broader moral reading: opening the gate is never an isolated act, because other people authorize it, assist it or bear its consequences. The phrase leaves “alone” open enough to trouble a person who wants sole blame assigned elsewhere. [B09-S3]

“Two witnesses required” gives a more countable procedure. But witnesses can observe without sharing the decision, ensuring a warning or preventing harm. The replacement may therefore narrow a demand for collective responsibility into a requirement for people who can attest that an event happened.

That is a leading interpretation, not proof of the narrator’s conscious motive. Requiring witnesses could also be a sincere attempt to improve accountability. The text resists accepting that explanation as complete through the old board: publicly replaced, it remains privately preserved under the bed, face down. Its imagined speech in Mara’s voice suggests that the discarded wording retains a claim on the narrator. Public praise for clarity does not quiet that claim. [B09-S3]

## Records supply different kinds of certainty

The official record dates the opening to noon. Memory dates it to Mara’s withdrawal of an answer. One gives clock time; the other organizes the event around a rupture between people. Neither identifies when they first heard the shouting, a chronology that might matter greatly to their opportunities to respond. Precision in one record does not supply the missing moral and causal sequence. [B09-S3]

The discarded ending, “Mara turned the wheel,” shows that an earlier version explicitly assigned the physical act. Its removal means the final passage withholds that assignment; the draft cannot be inserted back as a hidden fact. The jacket’s “confession of a coward” is a later marketing interpretation. It may fit the narrator’s evasions, but it cannot authenticate an admission the final text never makes. [B09-S4]

The resulting ambiguity is specific. Mara may have participated in the decision without being the sole initiator or operator. The narrator may be culpably evasive without having physically opened the gate. The brother’s fate, the warning’s timing and feasibility, and the actual operation remain unsettled. What the writing reveals most clearly is the narrator’s recurring effort to make selected signs answer more than they can.
