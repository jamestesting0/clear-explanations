**Both descriptions are arithmetically correct: the new workflow has a 10-percentage-point higher completion rate within each complexity group, yet its raw overall rate is 30 percentage points lower.** The workflows handled very different case mixes. Using the same complexity weights gives comparable descriptive summaries, but those summaries do not establish the causal effect of changing workflows. [A10-DATA; A10-ASSIGNMENT]

## 1. Rates within groups and in the observed workloads

The outcome is completion within seven calendar days.

| Pre-existing complexity | Old workflow | New workflow | New minus old |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Easy | 144 / 180 = **80%** | 18 / 20 = **90%** | **+10 percentage points** |
| Hard | 6 / 20 = **30%** | 72 / 180 = **40%** | **+10 percentage points** |

The raw workflow rates count all completed cases over all cases handled by that workflow:

- **Old:** (144 + 6) / (180 + 20) = 150 / 200 = **75%**.
- **New:** (18 + 72) / (20 + 180) = 90 / 200 = **45%**.
- **Raw contrast:** 45% − 75% = **−30 percentage points**. [A10-DATA]

Each raw rate is also a weighted average of its two group rates, using that workflow’s actual case mix:

| Summary | Easy weight | Hard weight | Calculation | Population described |
|---|---:|---:|---|---|
| Raw old | 90% | 10% | 0.90 × 80% + 0.10 × 30% = **75%** | The 200 cases actually assigned to old |
| Raw new | 10% | 90% | 0.10 × 90% + 0.90 × 40% = **45%** | The 200 cases actually assigned to new |

Hard cases have lower completion rates under both workflows, and they make up 90% of the new workflow’s workload but only 10% of the old workflow’s workload. Consequently, the new workflow’s overall average is dominated by its 40% hard-case rate, while the old workflow’s average is dominated by its 80% easy-case rate.

This reversal between within-group and overall comparisons is often called **Simpson’s paradox**. There is no arithmetic contradiction: the overall averages use different weights. [A10-DATA]

## 2. The two requested standardized comparisons

Standardization here means applying the **same chosen complexity weights** to each workflow’s observed group rates. It makes their recorded complexity mix comparable.

| Target case mix | Weights for both workflows: easy, hard | Old standardized rate | New standardized rate | New minus old |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Half easy, half hard | 0.50, 0.50 | 0.50 × 80% + 0.50 × 30% = **55%** | 0.50 × 90% + 0.50 × 40% = **65%** | **+10 percentage points** |
| Expected next-quarter mix | 0.20, 0.80 | 0.20 × 80% + 0.80 × 30% = **40%** | 0.20 × 90% + 0.80 × 40% = **50%** | **+10 percentage points** |

The first pair describes a common reference population with equal shares of easy and hard cases. In this dataset, that also matches the complexity mix of all 400 cases combined: 200 easy and 200 hard.

The second pair describes a reference population with the expected next-quarter complexity mix, under the committee’s convention of retaining the observed group rates. Its lower levels reflect the larger share of hard cases. The contrast stays at 10 percentage points because both group contrasts are 10 percentage points. [A10-DATA; A10-PLANNING]

These are descriptive reweightings. In particular, the next-quarter calculation does not establish that an actual rollout would preserve these rates when urgency, staffing, capacity, or other conditions change. [A10-PLANNING; A10-ASSIGNMENT]

## 3. Assessing the three claims

### The director: a real overall difference, an unsupported causal conclusion

The 30-percentage-point decline is correct for the two observed workloads. It can be useful for describing operational completion performance and workload composition.

It does not show that the new workflow caused harm. Assignment differed in complexity, and it also differed in urgency and specialist involvement. The raw groups therefore differ in more than workflow. [A10-DATA; A10-ASSIGNMENT]

### The implementation lead: higher group rates do not prove individual benefit

The new workflow’s observed rate is higher in both complexity groups. Standardizing on complexity preserves that advantage. But cases within a complexity group were still routed differently: urgency affected assignment and can affect completion, while the specialist worked exclusively with the new workflow. Staff differences may also matter. [A10-ASSIGNMENT]

Knowing that complexity preceded assignment avoids treating it as a consequence of the workflow; it does not establish that controlling for complexity removes all relevant differences between the groups.

The adjusted 10-percentage-point difference is therefore an **observed comparison**, not a proven effect of switching a particular case. For an individual case, the causal question compares its outcome under the new workflow with the outcome that same case would have had under the old workflow. The table observes different cases in those two conditions.

Even a well-established positive average causal effect would not show that every case benefits. Some cases could benefit, some could be unaffected, and some could be harmed while the average improves. The “patient” wording supplies no additional evidence; the units here are service cases. [A10-DATA; A10-CLAIMS]

### The analyst: an average needs a defined target

An unweighted average of all four group rates is:

(80% + 30% + 90% + 40%) / 4 = **60%**.

That gives each workflow–complexity cell weight 25%. It describes a constructed population split equally across those four cells, not a comparison between workflows. If the analyst instead means averaging the two complexity rates separately for each workflow, that produces the requested 55% versus 65% comparison.

The four-rate mean happens also to equal the pooled observed rate, (150 + 90) / 400 = 60%. But the actual pooled cell weights are 45%, 5%, 5%, and 45%, rather than 25% each. Their numerical agreement here does not justify averaging arbitrary subgroup percentages without specifying a population. [A10-DATA]

## 4. Evidence that would reduce uncertainty about rollout

The first decision is what intervention the committee wants to evaluate: **the workflow process itself, or the workflow together with its specialist staffing arrangement**. Those are different causal questions.

| Approach | What it can establish or improve | What still needs attention |
|---|---|---|
| Randomly assign eligible cases to workflows within easy and hard groups | Makes assignment independent of pre-existing case characteristics, including unrecorded urgency, in expectation; supports estimating average effects within complexity groups | Routing overrides can undermine the design. Exclusive specialist staffing would make the comparison a workflow-and-staffing package comparison. |
| Measure relevant pre-assignment variables and analyze comparable cases | Allows adjustment for recorded urgency and other reasons for routing, beyond complexity | Requires adequate overlap between workflows for comparable cases and an assumption that important remaining differences have been addressed. Measurement alone does not remove unrecorded causes. |
| Vary staffing separately from workflow | Can distinguish the workflow’s contribution from specialist or individual-staff contributions | Requires both workflows to be used across suitable staff arrangements, rather than keeping the specialist exclusive to new. |

A prospective randomized comparison would most directly address the routing uncertainty. Within each complexity group, allocate cases using a genuine random process rather than managerial preference; record urgency and other baseline predictors so that balance, treatment implementation, and differences in benefit can be assessed. Randomization balances such factors in expectation, not necessarily perfectly in a finite sample.

If the intended intervention includes the specialist, a trial of the whole package is relevant—but the committee must assess whether that staffing arrangement can be supplied at rollout scale. If the intended intervention is the workflow process independent of specialist access, have comparable staff use both workflows, randomize workflow within staff where feasible, or separately assign workflow and specialist availability. Such a design can also reveal whether the workflow works differently with specialist support.

Recording staff identity is useful, but it cannot by itself separate two factors that never vary independently. If the specialist remains exclusive to the new workflow, the table cannot tell how much of an advantage belongs to the workflow and how much to specialist involvement.

If randomization is infeasible, collect urgency, relevant baseline case characteristics, routing reasons, and staffing availability, then compare workflows among cases with comparable recorded circumstances. This improves the observational evidence, but its causal interpretation still depends on whether consequential unrecorded differences remain. Merely collecting a larger version of the existing table would improve precision without resolving those assignment problems. [A10-ASSIGNMENT]

For the rollout decision, translate credible group-specific causal estimates to the intended population using the 20% easy / 80% hard weights, while checking that its urgency and staffing conditions are represented. The raw rates, standardized descriptions, and causal estimates each answer useful questions; the error is treating one as if it automatically answered the others.
