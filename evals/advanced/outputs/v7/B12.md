The stipulated facts establish two successors with a shared remembered past and separate developing lives. They do not choose a criterion for being “the same person.” The community can nevertheless assign a definite project obligation because its rule defines authorized succession without requiring a verdict about personal identity. Remembering a promise is evidence of psychological continuity in this story. It is not automatically an exclusive title to Vale’s identity, property or obligations. The same memory exists in both successors, and neither receives uniquely privileged status from the technical report. [B12-S1–S3]

## Why branching complicates strict identity

**Numerical identity** means being literally one and the same individual, rather than two individuals that resemble each other. Ordinary identity is transitive: if East is identical to Vale, and West is identical to Vale, then East must be identical to West.

But East and West are stipulated to be distinct, independently acting successors. They occupy separate machines and acquire different experiences. Therefore both cannot be numerically identical to Vale while ordinary identity retains those properties. [B12-S1]

This does not establish which successor, if either, is strictly Vale. It shows why a relation that can branch—such as sharing memories and continuing projects—cannot simply be equated with ordinary numerical identity.

The proposed theories choose different standards or priorities:

- **Theory A** requires continuity of one physical implementation. Shared copied memories alone cannot establish that requirement.
- **Theory B** treats sufficient psychological continuity as what matters for surviving projects. It can recognize both successors as continuing Vale’s concerns without claiming that both are numerically the same individual as Vale.
- **Theory C** gives public recognition practices a role in constituting personal identity. But this community’s particular project rule explicitly declines to decide identity, so its adoption does not by itself establish Theory C.

The technical history supplies facts to which these theories would respond; it does not contain the further argument that selects among them. The brochure’s “you live twice” supplies no such argument either. [B12-S1, B12-S4]

## What the speakers actually disagree about

**East** moves from a true memory claim to an unsupported exclusive claim. East sincerely remembers accepting the advance, but West has the same remembered basis. Under the actual rule, East owes the project jointly with West, rather than alone. **West’s** objection successfully challenges East’s claimed evidential advantage. It does not prove that both are strictly identical to Vale. [B12-S1–S3]

**The neighbor** expresses a principle against multiplying what the community can demand merely because copying occurred. That principle matches the adopted rule. It is a choice about how obligations should carry forward, not a consequence that settles metaphysical identity. [B12-S2–S3]

**The treasurer** assumes that an obligation can survive only if someone is strictly the original promisor. That could be proposed as an alternative policy, but it misstates this community’s rule. Authorized successors owe the project whether or not either is numerically Vale. Even granting the treasurer’s premise that neither is strictly original would not trigger forgiveness under the supplied rule. [B12-S3]

**The spectator** makes an unsupported prediction about behavior. Identical inherited memories do not establish identical future choices when later information and experience can differ. East has already received information West lacks. That defeats the claim that shared memory proves future agreement; it does not prove that their next choices must differ. [B12-S1–S2]

These disputes therefore mix distinct issues: the spectator misuses facts about information and behavior; East overstates what memory establishes; the neighbor and treasurer invoke competing principles for distributing obligations.

## The practical result is one jointly owed performance

Vale’s documented continuity plan named both East and West, so both meet the rule’s definition of authorized successor. Together they owe **one complete community map or one full repayment of the advance**. They may agree on their internal division of work or payment. The community cannot demand a second full performance after receiving the first. [B12-S3]

The rule does not prescribe an equal split, identify which successor owns all of Vale’s property, or authorize punishment. Nor does making both responsible for completing the project prove that they are one person. A rule can allocate duties between distinct successors precisely because a continuing project needs a practical resolution while identity remains disputed.

## Later blame needs its own grounds

If East later does something wrongful, assessing West’s blame would require further facts: Did West participate, authorize or knowingly assist? Did West know enough to intervene, have the ability to do so, and have an applicable duty? What standard connects those facts to blame?

Shared pre-copying memory establishes none of those conditions for a later act. West does not automatically acquire East’s later knowledge, intentions or control, and common origin does not itself make East’s action West’s action. Conversely, separate identity would not excuse West from a later act that West actually helped bring about.

The project rule allocates a forward-looking obligation to deliver work or repayment. It supplies no general theory of culpability, punishment or unrelated ownership. Vale’s earlier cartography award adds nothing about those missing rules or the copying event. [B12-S1, B12-S3–S4]
