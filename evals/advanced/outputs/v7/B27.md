## How agreement changes its object

The Clerk's “something must be done” is broad enough to attract agreement without specifying what should happen. The Carpenter starts to agree about “the roof—”, but the bell ends the sentence before he can complete or qualify it. “Agreement recorded” turns that unfinished contribution into a completed administrative fact. When the Carpenter later agrees that they cannot wait all winter, the same phrase absorbs agreement about urgency into the record again. Neither statement, as heard, establishes assent to every provision of the motion. [B27-S1, B27-S2]

That distinction matters because the motion moves from repairing the roof to removing the east wall. The audience cannot hear three intervening lines. It therefore cannot establish how those actions are connected or assess the whole proposal; the direction does not establish what the characters themselves heard. The wet page and the Clerk's claim that only the dry part is affordable also connect access to the proposal with material scarcity. What can be read becomes entangled with what can supposedly be done. [B27-S1, B27-S2]

The YES cards make assent visible before deliberation has established its meaning. The actors place them on the table, but the cards were already supplied on their chairs. This makes agreement look both performed by the participants and prepared for them. The cupboard labeled OTHER CARDS suggests alternatives without revealing its contents or making those alternatives available. These objects support a reading of constrained participation; they do not prove that every actor privately disagrees. [B27-S1, B27-S2]

## What the minutes leave out

“All voices present agreed” narrows the relevant public to those counted as present and treats their contributions as agreement. “No objection was entered” describes the record, not necessarily the range of views people held or tried to express. The earlier interruptions show how a record can lack an objection when speech has not been allowed to reach a clear conclusion. No final vote is shown in the script. [B27-S1, B27-S2, B27-S3, B27-S4]

The moving chairs unsettle the apparent precision of “present.” As the original actors change seats during the reading, the official sentence claims a stable collective that the stage picture refuses to keep still. The Teacher's “Present when?” asks which moment establishes membership and consent. The empty chairs extend that question to people outside the counted group, without identifying a single fixed population they must represent. [B27-S1, B27-S3]

The child's blank card over a YES interrupts the visible tally without providing an unambiguous NO. It might suggest withheld consent, an unanswered question, or the inadequacy of the available choices. Its force comes partly from refusing the Clerk's ready-made wording. The script does not assign it one settled meaning. [B27-S2, B27-S4]

## Why one corrupt official is not enough

Procedural exclusion is a strong reading: the Clerk interrupts, records broadly, and controls access to the motion. But the production notes require him not to sneer, and the rain remains a practical problem for everyone. His actions can therefore be understood as harmful shortcuts under pressure without the performance certifying a simple motive of malice. The promotional description supplies a narrower interpretation than the performed text establishes. [B27-S1, B27-S2, B27-S4]

Likewise, the Treasurer holds the key but cites water beneath the cupboard. That may be obstruction or a genuine concern; the direction explicitly leaves it open. The discarded draft's accusation of dishonesty cannot settle the performed character's motive. The uncertainty keeps a practical question—whether opening the cupboard is safe—inside the political question of who controls alternatives. [B27-S2, B27-S4]

## Shelter and the limits of “we”

Lifting the table creates an actual act of cooperation: the place of decision becomes a makeshift roof. The falling YES cards loosen the connection between that cooperation and its earlier signs of assent. The Clerk shelters with the others, which places him inside their shared predicament. But the child is now outside. Collective action provides protection while distributing it unequally. [B27-S3]

The child's question makes that distribution central: who belongs to “we,” and how can a decision account for the empty chairs? The Clerk's sentence that will “hold water” promises a defensible record while everyone still needs literal shelter. The blackout supplies neither a complete motion nor a resolved membership rule. It leaves the audience with linked uncertainties about the proposal, the safety of alternatives, and whose agreement and protection a public decision must include. [B27-S3, B27-S4]
