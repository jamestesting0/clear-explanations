**A doubly positive part has a 26.87% probability of being defective under the supplied population and joint rates.** That is higher than the 15.52% probability after A alone, but far below the memo’s roughly 75%. B adds information; the memo overstates how much it adds after A has already been positive. [A09-BASE; A09-JOINT]

## Expected counts and probabilities

In 10,000 incoming parts, the expected numbers are:

- Defective: 10,000 × 0.02 = **200**.
- Nondefective: 10,000 × 0.98 = **9,800**.

Apply each joint rate to its own true-status group:

| Observed result | Expected defective parts | Expected nondefective parts | Expected total | Probability defective given that result |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| A positive, regardless of B | 200 × 0.90 = 180 | 9,800 × 0.10 = 980 | 1,160 | 180 / 1,160 = **15.52%** |
| A positive, B positive | 200 × 0.72 = 144 | 9,800 × 0.04 = 392 | 536 | 144 / 536 = **26.87%** |
| A positive, B negative | 200 × 0.18 = 36 | 9,800 × 0.06 = 588 | 624 | 36 / 624 = **5.77%** |

These are expected shipment counts, not a guarantee of the exact realized counts. The probability calculations use the known population rates. As a check, the two joint-result rows partition the A-positive group: 144 + 36 = 180 defective parts, and 392 + 588 = 980 nondefective parts. [A09-BASE; A09-JOINT]

The large nondefective population matters. Although only 4% of nondefective parts are doubly positive, that produces 392 expected parts, more than the 144 expected defective doubly positive parts.

## Exactly where the memo goes wrong

The marginal rates extracted from the table are correct:

- A: sensitivity 0.72 + 0.18 = **0.90**; false-positive rate 0.04 + 0.06 = **0.10**.
- B: sensitivity 0.72 + 0.08 = **0.80**; false-positive rate 0.04 + 0.01 = **0.05**.

Here, sensitivity means the positive rate among defective parts; the false-positive rate is the positive rate among nondefective parts. Neither is the probability that a positive part is defective. [A09-JOINT]

Multiplying marginal rates to obtain a joint rate requires the relevant independence relationship within each true-status group. For defective parts, the multiplication happens to agree with the supplied joint rate:

0.90 × 0.80 = 0.72.

For nondefective parts, it does not:

0.10 × 0.05 = 0.005, whereas the supplied doubly positive rate is **0.04**.

The memo therefore assumes only 9,800 × 0.005 = **49** nondefective doubly positive parts instead of **392**—an eightfold understatement. Its resulting calculation is:

144 / (144 + 49) = **74.61%**.

That explains the “roughly three-quarters” result, but its false-positive input contradicts the joint table. The correct denominator is 144 + 392. [A09-JOINT; A09-MEMO]

## B must be evaluated inside the A-positive group

Once A is positive, the relevant comparison is how often B is positive among defective versus nondefective **A-positive** parts:

| B’s positive rate within A-positive parts | Calculation | Result |
|---|---|---:|
| Among defective parts | 0.72 / 0.90 | **80%** |
| Among nondefective parts | 0.04 / 0.10 | **40%** |

A positive likelihood ratio compares those two rates. Within this selected group, B’s positive likelihood ratio is therefore:

**0.80 / 0.40 = 2**, not 16.

It multiplies the odds of a defect by two. Odds compare defective to nondefective parts: after A alone they are 180/980; after B is also positive they are 144/392, exactly twice as large. Doubling odds does not double probability—the probability rises from 15.52% to 26.87%. [A09-JOINT]

The marginal ratio 0.80/0.05 = 16 is valid for B used without first conditioning on A’s result. It is not B’s additional likelihood ratio after A has selected a group in which nondefective parts are much more likely to trigger B.

A negative B result also supplies information within A-positive parts. Its rates are 0.18/0.90 = 20% among defective parts and 0.06/0.10 = 60% among nondefective parts. The negative likelihood ratio is 0.20/0.60 = **1/3**, reducing the odds after A alone to one-third of their previous value and yielding the **5.77%** probability above.

Thus the second analyst’s “literally no extra information” claim is also wrong. B distinguishes true-status groups within the A-positive subset: its positive rate is 80% versus 40%. Dependence between detectors does not make one completely uninformative. [A09-JOINT; A09-MEMO]

Running B only after A changes which results production observes, not the underlying outcomes. The separate study that tested both detectors and obtained true labels supports these conditional calculations; no assumption about unobserved production B results is needed. [A09-BASE; A09-JOINT]

## Turning probabilities into inspection decisions

These probabilities estimate defect risk; they do not specify the best action. A defensible policy still needs the consequences and cost of a missed defect, the cost and consequences of inspecting a sound part, the effectiveness of manual inspection, and available inspection capacity. Those determine whether inspecting a group has enough expected benefit and how to prioritize scarce inspection slots.

For example, doubly positive parts have higher estimated risk than A-positive/B-negative parts. That supports prioritizing them when inspection costs and benefits are otherwise comparable; it does not establish that the lower-risk group should be ignored or that 50% is an appropriate cutoff. Defect status, rather than agreement between detectors, remains the target.

If the incoming defect rate changes while the joint conditional rates remain applicable, the probabilities and expected counts must be recalculated using the new rate. For prevalence p, the doubly positive probability becomes:

P(defective | A+, B+) = (0.72p) / [0.72p + 0.04(1 − p)].

If detector behavior, operating conditions, or the mix of parts within either true-status group changes, the joint conditional rates themselves need fresh validation. That validation must retain reliable true-status labels and represent the incoming defective and nondefective groups; changes in dependence between A and B matter alongside changes in their marginal rates. Updating prevalence alone cannot repair obsolete detector-performance estimates. [A09-BASE; A09-JOINT]
