**The dashboard can show fast completions while a substantial group of jobs remains stuck.** Its latency histogram describes jobs that finished; the unfinished-job gauge also counts jobs that have not finished, including those awaiting the dependency. The supplied evidence does not yet justify doubling the workers. [B08-S1–S3]

## The job counts reconcile

The flow balance is:

**120 initially unfinished + 600 admitted − 540 completed = 180 finally unfinished.**

That agrees with the reported closing count. Because nothing was canceled, dropped, or transferred, the increase of 60 jobs is fully accounted for. [B08-S1]

| Quantity | Calculation | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Admission rate | 600 / 600 = **1 job/second** | Jobs entering the measured system |
| Completion rate | 540 / 600 = **0.9 jobs/second** | Jobs leaving through completion |
| Net accumulation | 60 / 600 = **0.1 job/second** | Average increase in unfinished work |
| Mean unfinished count | **150 jobs** | Time-weighted gauge measurement |

Calling 0.9 the arrival rate confuses departures with admissions. The mean gauge reading happens to equal the midpoint of 120 and 180, but the endpoints alone would not establish that mean; the continuously sampled gauge does. [B08-S1, B08-S4]

## The latency statistics describe a selected population

The 20-second mean and 25-second ninety-fifth percentile describe the **540 jobs that completed during this window**. Their recorded durations include their entire time since admission, even if admission preceded the window. The statistics exclude the 180 jobs still unfinished. They also do not describe precisely the 600 new admissions, because the completed group may include earlier arrivals. [B08-S2]

Thus the percentile cannot establish that 95% of all newly admitted jobs finished within 25 seconds. A responsive class can dominate the completed sample while a blocked class accumulates time without contributing any completed duration. That mechanism fits the observed dependency problem, although the missing class information prevents assigning every unfinished job to it. [B08-S2–S3]

## Why multiplying 1 by 20 does not test the gauge

The relationship the manager has heard is often called **Little’s law**: average unfinished work equals arrival rate times average time spent in the system. In its usual long-run application, the quantities must describe the same system and population, the averages must be well behaved, and jobs crossing the start and end of observation must not dominate the comparison. Stable long-run flow also makes admission and completion rates converge.

Here, the population is accumulating, the observation lasts only ten minutes, and the 20-second mean selects completions. Jobs already present at the start and still present at the end create substantial boundary effects. Multiplying the admission rate by that selected mean therefore does not establish an expected gauge value of 20. Likewise, 150 / 1 = 150 seconds would be a quotient, not a justified measurement of the eventual average duration of these jobs.

There is nevertheless an exact finite-window accounting check. Each unfinished job contributes one “job-second” for every second it spends in the system during the window:

- The gauge implies **150 × 600 = 90,000 job-seconds** in total.
- The completed jobs’ full durations sum to **540 × 20 = 10,800 job-seconds**.
- Their contribution *inside this window* can be no greater than 10,800, because some recorded duration may precede it.
- Therefore jobs still unfinished at the end must contribute **at least 79,200 job-seconds**, averaging at least **440 seconds inside the window per remaining job**. [Calculated from B08-S1–S2]

That is a meaningful constraint. The summaries are compatible with a persistent blocked population; they would not be compatible with all 180 remaining jobs having arrived only moments before the end. The discrepancy alone falsifies neither instrument, but their underlying job histories still need to satisfy this accounting.

## What the metrics say about scaling

Forty configured workers do not imply that 150 unfinished jobs are competing for forty occupied execution slots. Workers can release a slot while an external request remains pending. CPU utilization of 22% likewise measures processor use, not the availability of slots or the dependency’s capacity. [B08-S3]

More workers could help if otherwise runnable jobs spend substantial time waiting for slots. They could have little effect if the limiting step is a nonresponsive dependency, and might worsen throttling if that dependency cannot accept more concurrent requests. The present data do not distinguish those possibilities well enough to approve a doubling.

The proposed drain time, **180 / 0.9 = 200 seconds**, is a constant-rate projection after admissions stop. It is not an exact prediction: the remaining jobs may be disproportionately blocked, and 0.9 jobs/second is an observed rate rather than demonstrated capacity for that remaining mix. [B08-S3–S4]

The most useful next measurements are:

1. **Job histories and age by class:** admission, execution, dependency-wait and completion times, including jobs already present and those still unfinished. Follow the unfinished cohort to resolution.
2. **Actual worker demand:** occupied and available slots, plus the number and waiting time of jobs ready to execute. This separates waiting for a worker from waiting for something a worker cannot fix.
3. **Dependency behavior by class and time:** outstanding requests, response times, timeouts, errors and rate-limit responses, matched to the affected jobs.

Then run a bounded worker-count comparison with a comparable workload and measured dependency conditions. An increase in throughput and a reduction in waiting for slots would support scaling; continued dependency waiting would direct the remedy elsewhere. The quarterly uptime claim supplies no evidence about either mechanism in this window. [B08-S3–S4]
