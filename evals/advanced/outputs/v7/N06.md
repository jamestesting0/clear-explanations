The **95% describes how reliably the method produces intervals that contain the answer across repeated samples**.

Imagine estimating the average commute time for everyone in a city. There is one true average, even though you do not know it. That is the “fixed parameter”: the unknown number you are trying to estimate.

Different random samples give different estimates and therefore different intervals. If you repeatedly took new samples and used a valid 95% confidence-interval method, about 95% of the resulting intervals would contain that same true average. This depends on the method's sampling and statistical assumptions holding.

Now suppose your actual sample produces **20–30 minutes**. That is the “realized interval”: simply the interval your data produced.

The true average either falls within 20–30 minutes or it does not. You still do not know which. What the method gives you is its long-run success rate; it does not calculate a 95% probability for the true average being inside this particular interval.

A useful way to read your result is: **“My interval is 20–30 minutes, produced by a method that captures the true average about 95% of the time over repeated samples.”**
