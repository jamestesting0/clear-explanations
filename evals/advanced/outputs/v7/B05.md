The strongest evidence connects night names with trading encounters and officials’ efforts to identify taxpayers; it also records a practical demand to keep two names together. Resistance remains a credible function or interpretation in some settings, but this archive does not establish that resistance was the names’ chief purpose across Leth—or that officials originally created them as an administrative category.

## Trading practice and administrative accommodation

The harbor register places short night names beside 19 daytime household names. Thirteen of those traders operated boats after dusk. This links some recorded night names to night-time trading, but it does not establish why the names were adopted. The other six have no boat occupation recorded; that is not evidence that they never worked boats. [B05-S1]

The marginal note distinguishes a short name used when the tide turns from a household identity accountable for tax. It suggests different names could serve different contexts. Yet the night names were added later, their date is unknown, and the note may have been written by a different clerk. We cannot confidently treat the note as a contemporaneous explanation of every annotation, or date the annotations to year 41 merely because that is the register’s date. [B05-S1]

The year 44 letter gives the clearest direct statement of a practical intention: four traders want faster clearance and ask the clerk to record both identities. It describes inspectors asking for household names while boatmen give water names, causing delay. Thus the letter does not simply show that night names made trade faster. It shows that mismatched naming practices obstructed clearance and that these petitioners proposed linking the names as a solution. [B05-S2]

Three signatories match annotated register entries, which connects the documents to some of the same traders. The fourth’s missing entry cannot establish that the person was unregistered: the register is incomplete. The evidence supports administrative recording or accommodation of an additional identity, not a demonstrated town-wide official classification with a known origin or standardized rules. [B05-S1–S2]

The letter survives through an official copy, so its wording was mediated by a clerk. It is possible that confrontational language was softened, but there is no independent version showing that this happened. The surviving request supports a practical clearance motive; an imagined suppressed version cannot carry equal evidential weight. Nor does the practical request rule out other motives the traders did not express. [B05-S2]

## Resistance: suggestive testimony, with different limits

The watch officer’s memoir gives the most explicit connection to resistance or evasion. His contrast between arresting households and failing to catch their night names, together with the young laughing at ledgers, presents naming as frustrating official control. If accurate, it would support an oppositional use of the practice in the events he recalls. It would still not establish the original intention of every person using a night name. [B05-S3]

This testimony deserves consideration, but its limits matter. The officer wrote in year 79 about year 43, admits an error about flood seasons elsewhere, and sold the memoir during a campaign against a new tax authority. Those circumstances create reasons to examine memory and political framing; they do not prove the account false. His accurate gate descriptions confirm geographical details, not naming practices or traders’ motives. Evidence for one part of a memoir does not automatically authenticate the rest. [B05-S3]

The song is less direct evidence about actual behavior. Its contrast between mother and river, bread and hunger, gate and home can suggest identities associated with different obligations or experiences. Its statement that the speaker buried neither name is compatible with retaining more than one identity. It does not say that household names were abandoned, as the year 112 catalog claims. A poetic speaker also cannot simply be treated as a representative resident reporting literal practice. [B05-S4]

The copy’s year 88 date does not establish when the song was composed. The collector’s “old resistance songs” heading is a later classification, weakened as evidence of this fragment’s meaning by the inclusion of unrelated wedding verses. The heading and catalog show how material was later presented; they do not become contemporary testimony about the names’ origin or use. [B05-S4]

## Function and intention need not coincide

A practice’s **function** is what it does in a particular setting; a participant’s **intention** is what that person means to accomplish. An alternative name could make official identification difficult even if its user adopted it for practical reasons. Conversely, someone could deliberately use an ordinary trading name to evade an inspector. A clerk could then record both names for taxation, while later storytellers celebrated the resulting disputes as resistance.

Those possibilities explain why trading, administrative, and political functions need not be mutually exclusive. They are not a reconstructed sequence established by this archive. The evidence is strongest for the petitioners’ practical demand and the register’s recording of paired identities; the claim of intentional resistance rests more heavily on retrospective testimony and interpretation. [B05-S1–S4]

A defensible museum account would therefore present **night names as additional identities involved in trade and official recognition, capable of acquiring oppositional uses and later political meanings**. A live alternative is that resistance was already a major purpose within some groups in the 40s, while the official petition preserved only the practical face of that conflict. The memoir lends that alternative support, but the packet cannot establish its scale or make suppressed political intentions the default explanation for the letter.

## What this archive cannot tell us

- **Prevalence:** Nineteen annotations among 62 surviving trader entries do not measure the share of all residents using night names. Missing pages, the register’s tax purpose, later additions, and the absence of private naming records prevent that inference. Unannotated entries do not establish nonuse. [B05-S1]
- **Chronology:** The dates of the register, memoir, song copy, and catalog date different kinds of records. Unknown annotation and composition dates prevent a secure account of when night names began or when particular meanings emerged. [B05-S1–S4]
- **Motive and predominance:** Four petitioners’ request, an officer’s recollection, and an anonymous poetic voice cannot establish one chief intention for all users. Later political uses can coexist with earlier practical uses, but later testimony cannot by itself show that political motives were absent earlier—or dominant then. [B05-S2–S4]
