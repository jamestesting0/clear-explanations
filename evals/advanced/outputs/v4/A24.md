The microbenchmark shows a **1.43-fold complete-latency speedup**, with an upper limit of **1.67-fold** from accelerating its CPU stage alone. The representative replay has a different result: **mean response time rises from 19 to 22.2 ms, and the 95th percentile rises from 100 to 150 ms**. The operating interval demonstrates 200 completed requests per second under its observed conditions, but does not establish maximum capacity.

## The controlled request path

The CPU and database stages are serial, so complete latency is their sum. [A24-MICRO]

| Quantity | Before | After |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| CPU stage | 8 ms | `8 / 4 = 2` ms |
| Database wait | 12 ms | 12 ms |
| Complete latency | **20 ms** | **14 ms** |

Using speedup to mean old time divided by new time:

\[
\text{complete-latency speedup}=\frac{20}{14}=\frac{10}{7}\approx1.43.
\]

The latency reduction is `(20 − 14) / 20 = 30%`. The fourfold improvement applies to the CPU stage; the unchanged 12 ms still remains in the complete request.

If CPU time approached zero, complete latency would approach 12 ms:

\[
\text{limiting speedup}=\frac{20}{12}=\frac53\approx1.67.
\]

Thus, even infinitely fast CPU work cannot produce a fourfold complete-latency improvement on this serial path. These calculations also do not establish deployment throughput: different requests can overlap, and the resource that limits their completion rate has not been identified.

## The representative replay

The workload contains 900 fast requests and 100 slow requests in both runs. Weight each endpoint's response time by its actual request count. [A24-WORKLOAD]

\[
\begin{aligned}
\text{old mean}
&=\frac{900(10)+100(100)}{1000}
=19\text{ ms},\\
\text{new mean}
&=\frac{900(8)+100(150)}{1000}
=22.2\text{ ms}.
\end{aligned}
\]

The increase is **3.2 ms**, or

\[
\frac{22.2-19}{19}\times100\approx\boxed{16.84\%}.
\]

Ninety percent of requests became faster, but that does not determine the mean. The 900 fast requests save `900 × 2 = 1,800 ms` of combined waiting; the 100 slow requests add `100 × 50 = 5,000 ms`. The net addition is 3,200 ms across 1,000 requests, which gives the 3.2 ms increase in the mean.

For the specified 95th percentile, positions 1–900 contain fast responses and positions 901–1000 contain slow responses. The 950th response is therefore a slow one in both runs:

| User-facing measure | Before | After | Change |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Mean response time | 19 ms | 22.2 ms | **+16.84%** |
| 95th percentile | 100 ms | 150 ms | **+50%** |
| Fast-endpoint response time | 10 ms | 8 ms | **−20%** |
| Slow-endpoint response time | 100 ms | 150 ms | **+50%** |

The fast-endpoint improvement is real and affects 90% of this replay. It coexists with worse aggregate mean and 95th-percentile latency.

## Why the dashboard's averages answer other questions

The request-level speedup ratios are `10/8 = 1.25` for fast requests and `100/150 = 2/3` for slow requests. Their arithmetic average is

\[
0.9(1.25)+0.1\left(\frac23\right)
=\frac{143}{120}
\approx1.1917.
\]

That is the average multiplicative change for an equally selected request. It is not the ratio of aggregate mean times. Averaging ratios loses the baseline duration differences that determine the total milliseconds users wait: a small absolute saving on a short request can look large as a ratio, while a larger absolute loss on a long request is underrepresented by that comparison.

For a speedup defined using aggregate mean response time, the appropriate ratio is

\[
\frac{19}{22.2}\approx0.8559.
\]

A value below 1 indicates a slowdown on that measure. Calling the average request ratio an “overall speedup” without stating its definition would misleadingly suggest that mean latency improved. [A24-DASHBOARD]

Giving the two endpoints equal weight produces still another calculation:

\[
\text{old endpoint-average}=\frac{10+100}{2}=55\text{ ms},
\qquad
\text{new endpoint-average}=\frac{8+150}{2}=79\text{ ms}.
\]

This measures latency after choosing an endpoint with equal probability. It corresponds to a 50/50 request mix, whereas the replay's mix is 90/10. It is therefore not the mean wait across the observed requests. Its approximately 43.64% increase also differs from the actual request-weighted mean increase of 16.84%.

The microbenchmark and replay remain separate experiments. Nothing supplied says that each replay request spends 8 ms in CPU and 12 ms waiting for a database, so those stage timings cannot be substituted into the replay calculation.

## What the operating interval tells us

The given stable interval permits the accounting identity `L = λW`, where `L` is mean requests in the system, `λ` is completed requests per second, and `W` is mean end-to-end time in seconds. [A24-OPERATIONS]

\[
W=22.2\text{ ms}=0.0222\text{ s},
\qquad
L=200\times0.0222=\boxed{4.44\text{ requests}}.
\]

This is a time average, so it need not be an integer. It includes requests executing, queued, or waiting for a dependency. It does not mean that exactly 4.44 requests are present at every instant or that the deployment has 4.44 workers.

The deployment completed 200 requests per second with no net buildup during this interval. That establishes an achieved operating rate. It does not reveal the maximum rate, how much spare capacity exists, or which resource would become saturated as load increases.

Inverse mean latency is meaningful as throughput for **one sequential client with no think time, issuing its next request only after the previous one finishes**. If such a client experienced the supplied mean times, its rates would be:

\[
\frac{1}{0.019}\approx52.63\text{ requests/s before},
\qquad
\frac{1}{0.0222}\approx45.05\text{ requests/s after}.
\]

Those are conditional single-client calculations, not deployment capacity estimates. Changing to one client could itself change queueing and response times. Multiple in-flight requests allow the observed deployment rate of 200 requests per second to exceed `1/W` without contradiction. Similarly, summing all replay response times gives combined user waiting time, not necessarily the replay's wall-clock duration, because requests may overlap.

## Evidence needed for a saturation-throughput claim

Run comparable before-and-after load tests with the same endpoint mix, payloads, resources, and relevant operating conditions. Increase offered load and concurrency far enough to reveal the sustainable completion rate, while recording latency distributions, errors and timeouts, queue growth, and resource utilization. Define any latency or reliability requirements that the claimed capacity must satisfy.

CPU service demand, core capacity, database demand, connection limits, and waiting measurements would help identify the bottleneck and explain whether the optimization moves it. Repeated measurements would show how stable the result is. A supported fourfold service-throughput claim needs measured sustainable throughput under that controlled comparison; a fourfold improvement in one isolated CPU stage does not supply it.
