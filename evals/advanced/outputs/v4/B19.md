A cause can start a process that later keeps going through a different mechanism. In this device, the proposed roles are distinct: **S starts A, the A–B feedback supports continued activity, and old reporter R can remain visible even after activity ends.**

### Starting and maintaining the state

The device begins with inactive A already present. S activates it; active A promotes B production; B then helps keep A active. This reinforcing loop is called **positive feedback**. It explains how S can remain part of the causal history without needing to remain present throughout maintenance. It does not explain A or B appearing from nothing. [B19-S1]

The direct activity measurements support the distinction between starting and continuing. Devices given S stayed active for two hours after its removal, whereas unstimulated devices remained inactive during the observation period. Thus S was required to start devices from the stated resting preparation under the tested interventions. The experiments do not establish that no alternative starting route could ever work. [B19-S2]

Without B, devices still responded initially to S but lost A activity after S was removed. This supports B having a necessary role in obtaining sustained activity under these conditions, while showing that an early response to S can occur without B. Controls rule out changes in the measured initial A amount and S sensor as explanations. However, deleting B before stimulation does not cleanly distinguish a role in **establishing** the lasting state from a continuing role in **maintaining** an established state, nor does it uniquely prove every step of the proposed feedback mechanism. [B19-S2]

Removing B only after the switch is established would sharpen that distinction. If selective B removal caused direct A activity to fall while matched controls stayed active, that would strengthen the claim that established activity still depends on B. This experiment is planned, not completed. [B19-S4]

### What the remaining fluorescence means

R is an accumulated amount, not an instantaneous measurement of A activity. When production stops, existing R continues fluorescing while its amount declines. Starting from 100 units, thirty minutes covers three ten-minute halvings:

\[
100 \times \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^3 = 12.5\text{ units}.
\]

The sequence is 100 → 50 → 25 → 12.5 units. Thus substantial residual fluorescence is expected even with **no new production**. [B19-S1, B19-S3]

The observed 13 units are 0.5 unit above that prediction. No measurement precision or uncertainty is supplied, so the packet cannot establish whether this difference is meaningful or claim exact agreement. A predicted fractional number of fluorescence units is perfectly valid; the noninteger result does not itself refute the decay calibration.

Neither analyst's mechanistic conclusion follows:

- Remaining fluorescence does not prove that feedback or A activity continued; old R alone could account for nearly this amount.
- Declining fluorescence does not prove direct inhibition of A. Reporter production might be affected while A remains active, and the inhibitor's other effects inside the complete device are unknown.

The cell-free validation does not establish the inhibitor's full action inside the device. With no direct A measurement in this batch, the results cannot determine A's state, prove complete cessation of reporter production, or identify the inhibitor's mechanism. Measuring newly made R separately from old R while directly measuring A would address these ambiguities. [B19-S3–S4]

Finally, two hours of persistence support a limited observation, not a “perpetual memory machine.” The experiments do not establish indefinite activity or the energy and material supplies needed to sustain it. A packaging award provides no evidence on either question. [B19-S4]
