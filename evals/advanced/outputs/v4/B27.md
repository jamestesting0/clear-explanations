The scene makes unanimity through gestures, interruption, and official wording before it establishes agreement on a complete proposal. Everyone has reason to want shelter, but the Clerk repeatedly turns that shared urgency into a record that appears to authorize something more specific. The rain makes this conversion both politically troubling and practically understandable.

## Agreement arrives before a clear question

The opening YES cards give agreement a visible form before the motion is read. The actors do place the cards themselves, but the cards have already been selected for them: they were on their chairs when the lights rose. Other cards exist, yet remain in a closed cupboard. This supports a reading in which the procedure makes assent readily available while making alternatives difficult to express. It does not establish what every actor privately believes or whether a final vote ever occurs (B27-S1).

The twelve chairs also make the five original participants visibly incomplete as a gathering. The empty places put pressure on the Clerk's opening “We have all agreed”: who belongs to “we,” and whose absence can the procedure disregard?

The Carpenter's unfinished “I agree that the roof—” exposes a crucial gap between using the word *agree* and agreeing to a specified decision. The bell prevents the audience from hearing the rest; “Agreement recorded” supplies closure in its place. Later, “I agree we cannot wait all winter” establishes urgency, but does not necessarily approve removing the east wall. Repeating “Agreement recorded” makes these different statements look procedurally equivalent (B27-S1–S2).

The motion itself cannot close that gap. The audience hears roof repair at one end and removal of the east wall at the other, but rain obscures three intervening lines. The production therefore withholds the very connection needed to assess the proposal. Neither the participants' full understanding nor the proposal's practical merits can be reconstructed with confidence from what the audience hears.

## The record determines what counts

The Teacher tries to inspect both the motion and the available alternatives. Each attempt encounters a material obstacle: the wet page, the closed cupboard, the key that is displayed but not used. “The dry part is the part we can afford” makes the damaged document stand for an economic limit, but also permits that limit to determine how much of the proposal can be examined. Practical scarcity and control of discussion become difficult to separate.

The final minutes narrow agreement further: “All voices present agreed. No objection was entered.” The second sentence describes what reached the record. It cannot establish that no one had an objection, especially when questions and unfinished statements have repeatedly failed to receive an answer. The first sentence depends on which voices count as present and when their presence counts.

As the five actors change chairs during the reading, their movement unsettles the apparent stability of that membership. It does not create new voters or prove that a hidden vote happened. The Teacher's “Present when?” instead exposes the missing definition that the confident sentence conceals (B27-S3).

The child's blank card complicates the visible YES without providing a determinate NO. It could suggest refusal, uncertainty, or a position the available procedure cannot express. Its force comes partly from that openness; the scene does not supply a fixed dictionary for its objects.

## Exclusion without a complete villain explanation

A strong reading of procedural exclusion follows from these details: alternatives remain inaccessible, distinct kinds of assent are collapsed, and the written record presents an unsettled process as unanimous. The Clerk has particular power over those conversions.

Yet the production instructions resist making that power explain everything. The Clerk must not sneer; the rain is a real problem for everyone. The Treasurer's concern about water beneath the cupboard may be an excuse, a genuine fear, or a mixture that a production must handle without textual certainty. The discarded draft's accusation of dishonesty is absent from the performed version. Likewise, the publisher's “simple exposure of a corrupt official” offers a promotional interpretation, not an event or motive established by the scene (B27-S4).

The Clerk's wish for “a sentence that would hold water” joins the desire for a defensible record to the immediate need for shelter. That connection explains the appeal of closure without proving that the closure is justified.

## Who receives shelter?

Lifting the table finally produces collective action and a makeshift roof. The falling YES cards expose the fragility of the earlier display, though their fall need not mean that every actor deliberately withdraws consent. The Clerk shelters with the others, sharing the practical emergency.

The child, however, is outside. The final arrangement gives the question about “we” a physical consequence: a group can cooperate successfully while excluding someone from its protection. The empty chairs extend that question to people whose presence and claims were never settled. They need not stand for one specific absent group to make the boundary visible.

The blackout leaves both practical and political questions open: what the unheard motion actually proposed, whether opening the cupboard was safe, whose assent the record could legitimately claim, and who should have been included in the shelter. The scene's force depends on preserving those gaps rather than supplying a missing vote or a final verdict on every character.
