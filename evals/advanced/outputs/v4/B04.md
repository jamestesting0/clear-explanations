The trial recorded less expired stock and higher aggregate contribution, but it does not establish that the forecasting system caused either improvement or preserved customer service. North sold a greater share of its deliveries while spending much more time out of stock—a result that shows why the report's “service rate” cannot answer the service question.

## What the arithmetic actually shows

Across both stores, receipts stayed at 1,400 items. Sales rose from 1,260 to 1,330, while expired discards fell from 140 to 70. With no opening or closing inventory and no transfers, the ledger accounts for all received items. [B04-S1]

Waste therefore fell by 70 items, or 50 percent. As a share of receipts, it fell from 10 percent to 5 percent, a reduction of five percentage points. This is a measured change in waste, not a 50 percent improvement in forecasting accuracy. The packet supplies neither forecasts nor the underlying customer demand against which to measure forecast errors.

Sales divided by receipts rose from 90 percent to 95 percent in **each store** and in aggregate. This measures the share of delivered stock that sold, usually called sell-through. It does not measure the share of customers' desired purchases that the stores fulfilled. Ordering too little can increase sell-through while disappointing more customers: nearly everything delivered may sell, yet additional customers may find empty shelves.

The store-level changes matter:

| Store | Receipts | Sales | Expired discards | Hours without stock |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| North | 1,000 → 800 | 900 → 760 | 100 → 40 | 2 → 18 |
| South | 400 → 600 | 360 → 570 | 40 → 30 | 1 → 1 |

North's unavailable time rose from 1 percent to 9 percent of its 200 opening hours. South remained at 0.5 percent. These logs establish worse availability over time at North; they do not reveal how many customers encountered it. The two notes about long queues suggest potentially consequential shortages, but cannot supply a missed-purchase count. [B04-S2]

## Contribution increased by 700 currency units

Under the supplied accounting assumptions, contribution before disposal and other unchanged expenses is sales revenue minus the acquisition cost of **all items received**, including those later discarded:

\[
\text{Contribution}=10\times\text{items sold}-6\times\text{items received}.
\]

| Store | Baseline contribution | Trial contribution | Change |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| North | 9,000 − 6,000 = 3,000 | 7,600 − 4,800 = 2,800 | −200 |
| South | 3,600 − 2,400 = 1,200 | 5,700 − 3,600 = 2,100 | +900 |
| **Total** | **4,200** | **4,900** | **+700** |

Aggregate revenue increased from 12,600 to 13,300. Acquisition costs happened to remain at 8,400 because aggregate receipts were unchanged. Thus the revenue increase also became a 700 increase in contribution, about 16.7 percent. The finance analyst's favorable direction is supported by this accounting, but contribution is not total profit, and the calculation does not identify what caused the gain. [B04-S1]

## Several causal stories fit the results

**Better matching is possible.** The system may have improved ordering quantities or timing, leaving fewer items to expire and directing more deliveries toward a store with greater purchasing activity. But the records do not show forecast errors or delivery timing, so that mechanism remains a hypothesis.

**North may simply have ordered too little for parts of the period.** Its deliveries fell by 200 items, sales fell by 140, and discards fell by 60. Less supply can produce both lower waste and more stockout hours even if total demand stays constant or falls. Consequently, the manager cannot infer rising total demand from the increase in stockout hours. The bus-stop loss could also have changed North's customer traffic or the timing of purchases, but its actual effect is unmeasured. [B04-S1–S3]

**South had a new source of purchases.** Its cafeteria customer bought 120 items, while South's total sales increased by 210. Subtracting those purchases leaves 450 trial sales to other customers, compared with 360 baseline sales. That is a useful description, not a causal adjustment: without the cafeteria contract, ordering decisions and purchases by other customers might also have differed. The contract alone provides a reason to question attributing South's entire gain—or the aggregate gain—to forecasting. Receipts shifted toward South by 200 items while North received 200 fewer; no inter-store transfer is implied. [B04-S1, B04-S3]

Both stores changed systems at the same time, and there were no contemporaneous comparison stores. Temperature and events changed too. Selecting stores because baseline waste was unusually high adds another possibility: unusually bad periods may be followed by more ordinary periods even without an intervention. None of these alternatives proves the system ineffective; together they prevent a clean causal conclusion. [B04-S3]

The training data also contain a relevant limitation. Completed sales stop when stock is unavailable, even if customers still want the item. A model trained only on sales and deliveries may interpret those suppressed sales as low demand and recommend too little stock again. The packet makes that failure possible; it does not show that the system actually made this mistake. [B04-S2–S3]

## What cannot be recovered from these records

Unobserved demand means the purchases customers would have wanted to make if the item were available. The counter misses people who leave, substitute, or avoid entering. Stockout hours measure time without availability, not the number of desired purchases during that time. Without observations or justified assumptions about arrival rates and desired quantities, these records cannot yield a defensible numerical estimate—or a finite upper bound—for unmet demand. Multiplying average sales per hour by stockout hours would assume that demand during those hours resembled observed demand; the packet does not establish that assumption. [B04-S2]

Even a successful reconstruction would answer how much demand existed, not how stores would have performed during the same weeks without the forecasting system. The bus stop, cafeteria contract, weather, and selection of unusually wasteful baseline stores would still complicate attribution.

## A feasible expansion of the evaluation

Recruit stores with several customer patterns and randomly assign comparable stores either to adopt now or to continue their existing process during the same period. Group stores by features such as institutional customers and baseline availability before assignment, and record major local changes. This creates a contemporaneous comparison for the system's effect while testing whether results transfer beyond these two stores.

Track receipts and delivery timing, expiry, contribution, stockout periods, and directly observed requests that cannot be filled. Record substitutions and use sampled customer observations or surveys to investigate losses that checkout data miss. Keep uncertainty about deterred customers explicit.

Define success in advance as lower waste or higher contribution **with acceptable availability and fulfillment of desired purchases**. Lower waste alongside increased unmet requests would support underordering as the explanation. Improvements relative to comparison stores without degraded service would support better matching. Results concentrated in stores with new institutional customers would indicate that customer mix matters for expansion, rather than establishing a benefit for every store.
