A doubly positive part is **26.87% likely to be defective** under the supplied population and joint rates. That is more informative than A alone, which gives **15.52%**, but substantially below the memo's roughly 75%. B adds information within the A-positive group; its positive result multiplies the odds of a defect by **2**, not 16.

## Expected counts and probabilities

In 10,000 parts, a 2% defect rate gives an expected **200 defective** and **9,800 nondefective** parts. Applying the joint probabilities separately within those two true-status groups gives:

| Observed result | Expected defective parts | Expected nondefective parts | Expected total | Probability of a defect given the result |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| A positive, regardless of B | `200 × 0.90 = 180` | `9,800 × 0.10 = 980` | 1,160 | `180 / 1,160 = 15.52%` |
| A positive, B positive | `200 × 0.72 = 144` | `9,800 × 0.04 = 392` | 536 | `144 / 536 = 26.87%` |
| A positive, B negative | `200 × 0.18 = 36` | `9,800 × 0.06 = 588` | 624 | `36 / 624 = 5.77%` |

These are expected shipment counts, not a promise about the exact counts in a particular random shipment. The probabilities are population probabilities calculated using the stipulated exact rates. [A09-BASE, A09-JOINT]

For example, the doubly positive probability is the defective share of all doubly positive parts:

\[
P(\text{defective}\mid A+,B+)
=\frac{0.02\times0.72}
{0.02\times0.72+0.98\times0.04}
=\frac{144}{536}\approx26.87\%.
\]

Even though a defective part is much more likely to produce two positives, nondefective parts are far more numerous in the incoming population. That is why 392 of the expected 536 doubly positive parts are nondefective.

## Where the memo's multiplication fails

The extracted marginal rates are correct:

- A detects `0.72 + 0.18 = 0.90` of defective parts and fires on `0.04 + 0.06 = 0.10` of nondefective parts.
- B detects `0.72 + 0.08 = 0.80` of defective parts and fires on `0.04 + 0.01 = 0.05` of nondefective parts.

But multiplying marginal rates to obtain a joint rate requires independence within the relevant true-status group. The correct multiplication always uses B's rate **among the parts already selected by A**:

\[
P(A+,B+\mid\text{status})
=P(A+\mid\text{status})\,
P(B+\mid A+,\text{status}).
\]

For defective parts, the memo's product happens to match the table: `0.90 × 0.80 = 0.72`. For nondefective parts, it gives `0.10 × 0.05 = 0.005`, whereas the table gives **0.04**, eight times as much.

That error predicts only `9,800 × 0.005 = 49` nondefective doubly positive parts instead of 392. Using the incorrect denominator produces `144 / (144 + 49) = 74.61%`, locating the source of the memo's claim. The supplied table does not support that denominator.

## B's contribution after A is positive

Within the A-positive subset, B's relevant rates are:

\[
P(B+\mid A+,\text{defective})=\frac{0.72}{0.90}=0.80,
\]

\[
P(B+\mid A+,\text{nondefective})=\frac{0.04}{0.10}=0.40.
\]

The **positive likelihood ratio** is the ratio of those two probabilities. It measures how much a positive result multiplies the odds of a defect:

\[
LR_{B+\mid A+}=\frac{0.80}{0.40}=\mathbf{2}.
\]

After A alone, the odds of defective versus nondefective are `180:980`. Multiplying those odds by 2 gives `360:980`, equivalent to the doubly positive counts `144:392`. Converting back to a probability gives 26.87%. Doubling odds does not mean doubling probability.

The marginal ratio `0.80 / 0.05 = 16` describes B applied without first conditioning on A. It is not B's additional evidence after A has already selected a group in which B's nondefective positive rate is 40%.

B being negative also adds information: within A-positive parts, its negative rates are 20% for defective parts and 60% for nondefective parts. Their ratio is `0.20 / 0.60 = 1/3`, reducing the defect odds to one-third and yielding 5.77%.

Thus, the second analyst's “no extra information” claim is also wrong. Correlation can reduce the additional information without eliminating it. Here B separates the A-positive group into a 26.87% defect group and a 5.77% defect group.

Running B only after A changes which B results production observes; it does not change the underlying joint probabilities. The complete-label validation study supplies the joint information needed for these calculations. An unobserved B result on an A-negative part must not be treated as B-negative. [A09-BASE, A09-JOINT]

## Turning probabilities into an inspection policy

Neither 26.87% nor any other probability automatically determines whether inspection is worthwhile. The decision needs the consequences and costs of releasing a defective part, inspection costs and delays, the effectiveness of manual inspection and subsequent action, and available inspection capacity. Different defect severities could also imply different consequences at the same probability.

For illustration, if inspection perfectly detects and prevents a defect's consequences, costs \(I\) per part, and a missed defect causes a loss \(L\), inspecting a part with defect probability \(p\) would be worthwhile when \(pL>I\). Those are simplifying assumptions, not supplied facts. Capacity limits could require prioritizing parts by expected benefit even when several groups pass a cost threshold.

If the incoming defect rate changes, recompute these probabilities with that new rate; the current 2% baseline is part of every denominator. If detector behavior or the mix of part characteristics within defective and nondefective groups changes, revalidate the **joint** conditional table, not just the two marginal sensitivities. The validation must remain representative, use reliable true defect labels, and cover the relevant operating conditions. Sampling uncertainty can be assessed separately; the calculations here treat the supplied rates as exact, as requested.
