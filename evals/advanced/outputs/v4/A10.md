The new workflow has a **10-percentage-point higher observed completion rate within each complexity group**, yet a **30-percentage-point lower overall rate** because it handled a much harder case mix. Applying the same complexity weights to both workflows gives comparable descriptive summaries, but it does not remove the unrecorded urgency and staffing differences or establish the effect of switching a case's workflow.

## The observed rates and their weights

From [A10-DATA]:

| Summary | Old workflow | New workflow | New minus old |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Easy cases | `144 / 180 = 80%` | `18 / 20 = 90%` | **+10 percentage points** |
| Hard cases | `6 / 20 = 30%` | `72 / 180 = 40%` | **+10 percentage points** |
| All cases actually assigned | `150 / 200 = 75%` | `90 / 200 = 45%` | **−30 percentage points** |

The within-group comparisons describe the observed easy cases in each workflow and, separately, the observed hard cases in each workflow. They give each case equal weight within its group, without mixing complexity groups.

The two raw workflow rates use **different complexity weights**:

\[
\text{Old: }0.90(80\%)+0.10(30\%)=75\%,
\]

\[
\text{New: }0.10(90\%)+0.90(40\%)=45\%.
\]

The old workflow's observed population was 90% easy; the new workflow's was 90% hard. Since hard cases have much lower completion rates under either workflow, this shift in composition outweighs the higher observed rates within each group. This reversal between aggregate and within-group comparisons is often called Simpson's paradox.

A numerical decomposition makes the reversal concrete. Using the old workflow's rates with the new workflow's actual mix gives `0.10 × 80% + 0.90 × 30% = 35%`. Changing that mix lowers the descriptive rate from 75% to 35%, a 40-point decrease. Substituting the new workflow's within-group rates raises it from 35% to 45%, a 10-point increase. Together these yield the observed 30-point decline. This decomposition is arithmetic, not a causal attribution.

## The two requested standardized comparisons

A standardized rate is a weighted average of the group-specific rates using an explicitly chosen common case mix. It answers: “What average do these observed rates produce when summarized over this mix?”

| Target case mix | Old workflow calculation | New workflow calculation | New minus old |
| --- | --- | --- | ---: |
| 50% easy, 50% hard | `0.50 × 80% + 0.50 × 30% = 55%` | `0.50 × 90% + 0.50 × 40% = 65%` | **+10 percentage points** |
| 20% easy, 80% hard | `0.20 × 80% + 0.80 × 30% = 40%` | `0.20 × 90% + 0.80 × 40% = 50%` | **+10 percentage points** |

The first pair describes a common population containing equal shares of easy and hard cases. It removes the difference in the workflows' recorded complexity composition from this comparison.

The second pair describes the expected next-quarter mix, under the requested convention of using the currently observed group-specific rates. It is a planning summary, not proof that assigning next quarter's cases to either workflow would cause those rates. [A10-PLANNING]

Both differences are 10 points because the observed difference is 10 points in each group. The absolute rates differ because the second target population contains more hard cases.

## Assessing the three claims

**The director's arithmetic is correct; the claim of harm is not established.** The historical raw rate is 30 percentage points lower under the new workflow. That accurately describes outcomes for the cases each workflow actually received. Because those populations differ substantially, it does not isolate a workflow effect. Aggregate comparisons are useful for describing actual workload and performance; their causal interpretation needs more evidence.

**The implementation lead's within-group comparison is correct; the claim about every case is not established.** Both recorded complexity groups have a higher observed rate under the new workflow. However, within those groups, urgency influenced routing and can affect completion. The specialist worked exclusively with the new workflow, and other staff differences may matter. The table therefore compares cases and staff that may differ in ways relevant to the outcome. The direction or size of those influences is not supplied. [A10-ASSIGNMENT]

Complexity being measured before assignment makes it a relevant baseline variable, but does not establish that it accounts for every cause of both assignment and completion. Standardizing on complexity leaves those other differences unresolved.

Moreover, even a well-established positive **average causal effect** would not prove improvement for every individual case. For a particular case, we observe its outcome under the assigned workflow, not both its outcome under old and its outcome under new. Some cases might benefit, some might be unaffected, and others might fare worse while the average improves. These data concern service cases, not a separate patient population.

**“Average the four percentages” is incomplete without a target population.** Giving all four cells equal weight yields `(80% + 30% + 90% + 40%) / 4 = 60%`. That describes a hypothetical population assigning one-quarter of its weight to each workflow–complexity combination. It produces one combined rate, not a comparison between workflows.

Here 60% also happens to equal the pooled observed rate, `240 / 400`, despite the cells having unequal sizes. That coincidence does not justify unweighted averaging generally. For the committee's questions, the appropriate averages are the two separate workflow rates calculated with the stated common complexity weights.

## Evidence that would most directly support a rollout decision

The strongest next step is a prospective trial with **random assignment within complexity groups**, designed around the version of the service that would actually be rolled out. That would prevent the manager's preferential urgency routing from systematically choosing which cases receive each assigned treatment. Randomization balances recorded and unrecorded baseline causes in expectation; recording urgency remains useful for checking balance, improving precision, and studying whether results differ for urgent cases.

The staffing question determines what that trial estimates:

- **Keep the specialist exclusive to the new workflow:** randomize cases to the two complete service arrangements. This estimates the effect of assignment to the new workflow *with its staffing arrangement*. It can answer whether to roll out that package if the same staffing is feasible at scale.
- **Separate workflow from specialist involvement:** use a design in which comparable staff can deliver both workflows, or independently assign workflow and specialist involvement where feasible. This can distinguish a workflow effect from a staffing effect and reveal whether they work differently together. The current exclusive pairing cannot identify those components separately.
- **If randomization is infeasible:** measure relevant variables before assignment, including urgency, detailed case difficulty, and staffing or availability factors that influence routing and completion. Compare or adjust using these variables and the intended target mix. This can reduce confounding, but still depends on having measured the important common causes and having comparable cases available under both workflows.

These approaches answer related but different questions: whether the complete new service arrangement works, whether the workflow itself works across staff, and how much an observational comparison can account for known selection differences. Staff changes caused by the workflow may be part of the intervention being evaluated, so they should not automatically be adjusted away.

For the rollout decision, estimate the relevant effects in a population and staffing setting that can support the next-quarter 20% easy/80% hard mix, and report uncertainty. Some cells here contain only 20 cases. A larger repetition of the same routing pattern could improve precision, but would not by itself resolve urgency selection or separate the specialist's contribution from the workflow's.
