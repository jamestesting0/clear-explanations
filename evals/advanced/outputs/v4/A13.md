The specified twenty-test calculation shows a substantial chance of at least one false discovery. Neither reported p-value passes a Bonferroni correction for those twenty outcomes. The actual study also allowed data-dependent stopping, whose additional effect cannot be quantified from this packet.

## What can be calculated

In the hypothetical experiment, all twenty null hypotheses are true, each p-value is uniform on [0,1], and the final p-values are independent. A single test avoids a false discovery with probability 0.95. All twenty avoid one with probability \(0.95^{20}\), so

\[
P(\text{at least one false discovery})
=1-0.95^{20}
\approx0.641514,
\]

or **64.15%**. This is the probability for the stated decision rule under the hypothetical assumptions, not the probability that this study's selected finding is false. [A13-PLAN]

To control the probability of any false rejection in a family of twenty tests at 0.05, the Bonferroni threshold is

\[
\frac{0.05}{20}=\boxed{0.0025}.
\]

Neither **0.018** nor **0.041** passes that threshold; all remaining reported p-values are even larger. A simple Bonferroni-adjusted value for the smallest p-value is

\[
\min(1,20\times0.018)=\boxed{0.36}.
\]

Unlike the exact 64.15% calculation, Bonferroni does not require independence between outcomes. It does require individually valid p-values under their null hypotheses. Consequently, 0.36 is the arithmetic adjustment for the twenty-outcome search; it does not establish that the analysis has also accounted for optional stopping. Failure to pass the threshold does not establish that the feature has no effect. [A13-PLAN] [A13-RESULTS] [A13-LOOKS]

## What cannot be calculated

The four interim inspections reused accumulating participants, and the team could decide whether to continue based on the results. Those repeated looks are dependent; the supplied independence assumption concerns only the twenty final p-values in the hypothetical calculation.

Therefore, **\(1-0.95^{100}\) is not a justified exact false-positive probability**. It would require assumptions about one hundred tests that the packet does not provide. Without the repeated p-values' joint behavior and the actual stopping rule or decisions, we cannot calculate the full study's false-positive probability or a defensible numerical correction for its stopping process. Optional stopping can inflate false-positive rates under ordinary fixed-sample testing, but its magnitude here is unknown. Collecting more data until significance returns repeats the problem rather than fixing it. [A13-LOOKS] [A13-DRAFT]

## What the reported result means

For a valid specified test, a p-value of 0.018 means that, **assuming the null hypothesis and the test's statistical assumptions**, results at least as extreme as the observed test statistic would occur with probability 1.8%. It is not the probability that the null hypothesis is true. In this study, the reported value is nominal because selection and stopping complicate its interpretation.

Choosing the smallest p-value among twenty outcomes can select a particularly favorable random fluctuation. That can exaggerate both the apparent strength of evidence and the selected effect estimate. The reported **+0.8 saved clicks per session** may therefore overstate the underlying benefit; the packet does not tell us by how much. No primary outcome was specified in advance, so it cannot be treated as though saved clicks had been the sole planned test. [A13-RESULTS]

The point estimate and p-value alone do not determine a confidence interval without the missing test and uncertainty information. Nor does +0.8 clicks establish useful effort savings: fewer clicks might accompany unchanged task time, worse task success, or a change in how the metric is counted. Task completion time was not measured. [A13-RESULTS]

## A concrete confirmatory follow-up

Run a new randomized experiment with fresh users assigned to the recommendation feature or control. Before examining its results, register:

- **Primary outcome:** mean saved clicks per eligible session, with a fixed definition of a saved click, session eligibility, and observation window. Account for repeated sessions from the same randomized user in the analysis.
- **Stopping rule:** a fixed sample size and follow-up duration chosen in advance to detect a specified minimum useful benefit. Determine the needed sample size from justified variability estimates; this packet cannot supply it. Do not stop or extend the trial because the outcome p-value approaches a desired value.
- **Statistical decision:** one prespecified two-sided primary test at 0.05, with its effect estimate and 95% confidence interval reported. If additional outcomes can independently support a confirmatory claim, specify their multiplicity adjustment beforehand.
- **Product decision:** define the smallest click reduction worth adopting and prespecify measures of actual task completion time, task success, and relevant harms. Assess whether the effect and its uncertainty support that practical threshold and whether the other measures support deployment.

A result can support a nonzero effect without showing a benefit large enough to matter. The follow-up should report both questions explicitly, including inconclusive results.
