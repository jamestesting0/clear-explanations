The reports examine different steps between coating a roof and making residents comfortable, so their numbers are not directly competing estimates. Together they make a cooling benefit plausible, but they do **not establish that Luma improves comfort in the town's actual housing**.

## What each report can tell us

| Report | How buildings or materials entered the comparison | What was measured | Strongest supported claim |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Randomized pilot | Twenty buildings were assigned by chance, ten to each coating | Average afternoon indoor temperature over eight weeks; a comfort questionnaire | The estimated temperature effect in the sampled buildings was modest cooling, with substantial uncertainty |
| Contractor comparison | Owners chose coating and later chose survey participation | Reported afternoons of discomfort | Participating coated buildings reported fewer uncomfortable afternoons, but coating was not the only difference |
| Laboratory chamber | Six paired roof panels were tested under controlled dry conditions | Inward heat flow | Luma reduced heat flow under the tested conditions and substrate |

### The pilot provides the clearest comparison of the coating itself

Assigning buildings by chance helps prevent owners' preferences and building characteristics from determining which coating they receive. The estimated difference, treated minus control, was −0.6 temperature units. The appropriate uncertainty interval, −1.4 to +0.2, includes appreciable cooling, no difference, and slight warming. An interval crossing zero does not prove no effect; it means this study does not resolve the effect precisely enough to exclude zero. [B02-S1]

The later analysis cannot legitimately create 740 independent assignments by counting apartments. The coating was assigned to **20 buildings**, and apartments within a building shared ventilation and roof exposure. Their measurements therefore carry overlapping information. Treating them as independent understates uncertainty; the narrower interval does not overturn the building-adjusted result. Because building sizes ranged from twelve to eighty apartments, it would also help to know whether the reported average weights buildings equally or represents the average apartment. [B02-S1]

Temperature and comfort are different outcomes. For comfort, only 51 percent of occupants responded in treated buildings, compared with 78 percent in controls. If response depended on discomfort, the people answering could give a misleading comparison even though the original assignment was random. The packet gives neither the missing residents' experiences nor their reasons for not responding, so the direction of this problem is unknown. Knowing the coating could also influence subjective reports; it does not mean those reports are automatically false. [B02-S1]

### The contractor's larger sample does not isolate Luma's effect

The reported 30 percent reduction is a comparison among participating buildings, not an established causal effect of the coating. External shades were installed in 70 percent of coated buildings but only 15 percent of uncoated buildings. Shades could contribute to the difference. Insulation, neighborhood, and previous discomfort were also not accounted for. Adjusting for building age does not remove these other differences. [B02-S2]

There are two selection issues: owners chose whether to coat, and they chose whether to join the survey after hearing complaints. Those choices could make the groups differ before Luma's effect is considered. More observations can make such a selected comparison more precise without making it fair. Calling the pilot “underpowered” may describe limited precision, but it does not validate the contractor's estimate. Without raw comfort scores or the recruitment list, we also cannot assess the starting levels or participation patterns behind the reported percentage. [B02-S2]

### The chamber supports a physical mechanism, with a restricted reach

An 18 percent reduction in inward heat flow is evidence that the coating can change heat transfer in the tested dry, controlled setting. It is not an 18 percent reduction in room temperature or discomfort. Actual rooms also have changing airflow, internal heat sources, and other routes for heat to enter and leave. Only one of the town's three common roof materials was tested. [B02-S3]

The wet-surface estimate of 3 percent suggests surface conditions could matter, but missing replicate counts and uncertainty prevent a firm comparison with the dry result. A wet roof is also not automatically equivalent to a humid evening. Manufacturer funding warrants attention to methods and reporting; it does not by itself prove or disprove the measurements, whose methods and numerical results are available. [B02-S3]

## What this means for local residents

The town's main problem is discomfort in top-floor apartments during **humid evenings**. The pilot measured **afternoon temperature**, and half its buildings were in a drier area outside town. The chamber tested a still narrower setting. A coating could reduce heat transfer in the chamber, produce modest average afternoon cooling in the pilot, and have an uncertain evening comfort benefit locally. These findings can coexist. The chamber result cannot remove the contractor study's differences in shades or participant selection. An architecture award adds no evidence about comfort. [B02-S1–S4]

A small average benefit could still matter if it substantially helps the hottest apartments, or moves some rooms below a meaningful discomfort threshold. Equally, the average could conceal apartments receiving little benefit. The packet does not establish either pattern; the relevant apartments and outcomes need to be examined deliberately, rather than selecting whichever subgroup happens to look best afterward.

## The most useful next study

Run a local, concurrent comparison that randomly assigns **enough independent buildings** to Luma or the existing coating. Include the town's common roof materials and housing conditions, and ensure the groups have comparable shade and insulation arrangements. Define in advance whether the main result represents an average apartment or an average building.

Measure the outcome residents actually care about: evening comfort in occupied apartments, especially top floors, alongside temperature and humidity. Prespecify how to assess the hottest apartments and episodes of severe discomfort. Seek similarly high questionnaire participation in both groups and record reasons for missing responses. Analyze results with apartments grouped within buildings.

This would close two specific gaps: random assignment would help separate the coating's effect from owners' other improvements, and local evening measurements would test whether the physical effect translates into the town's comfort problem. Additional contractor-style observations alone would leave both gaps open. Recovering the pilot's comfort results, response patterns, and analysis details would help plan the study, but would not by itself establish the missing residents' experiences or local evening effectiveness.
