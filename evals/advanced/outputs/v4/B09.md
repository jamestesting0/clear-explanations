The passage makes Mara’s responsibility uncertain by showing how an accusation is assembled from evidence that will not quite support it. Its strongest implication is that the narrator wants to isolate Mara’s guilt while avoiding a more shared responsibility. But the narrator’s later exposure of that desire still does not establish who turned the wheel, what Mara authorized, or whether either person could have prevented harm.

## A memory organized around innocence

The opening reverses the expected order of reasoning: “I remember the gate opening because I remember being innocent.” Innocence appears to authorize the memory before the remembered event has been examined. The narrator sounds certain about a verdict while immediately becoming uncertain about the bell. The qualification “unless” and the possible “bell before the bell” unsettle the sequence on which judgments about warning and responsibility might depend. [B09-S1]

The missing boat repeats this pattern. Its absence becomes “proof” that the brother escaped, although it could support other possibilities. The retrospective admission that the narrator once “called many absences proof” invites distrust of the earlier reasoning. It also gives the older narrator an appearance of candor. That candor may help us recognize self-deception; it does not independently verify the corrected account or establish the brother’s fate.

The final movement separates clock time from remembered significance. The records place the opening at noon; memory places it at Mara’s withdrawal from conversation. The second account makes an event in their relationship into the event’s defining time. Neither account supplies the crucial sequence involving the shouting below. A record can give a precise time without answering who acted or what anyone knew. [B09-S3]

## Objects that invite accusation and then frustrate it

The key initially seems to assign control to Mara, but her reply redirects attention to the narrator’s empty belt and repeated reaching. The gesture could suggest habit, anxiety, or concealed involvement; it does not prove possession of the key. Similarly, hidden hands encourage suspicion before any action is seen. When Mara reveals a red palm, the wheel’s color makes contact seem possible, but the festival’s red handles supply other possible sources. The narrator’s sentence “I wanted the color to be an accusation” establishes that wish more securely than it establishes Mara’s act. [B09-S1–S2]

Other objects sharpen the difference between seeing and understanding. The water resembles an unopened letter: it can suggest significance awaiting recognition without telling us what anyone actually knew. The keeper’s lantern, lit or carried in daylight, adds no visibility. It may evoke the appearance of inspection or authority without additional evidence. The draft instruction to keep it unnecessary supports the deliberate preservation of that uselessness, not one compulsory symbolic meaning. [B09-S1–S2, B09-S4]

## Three questions of responsibility

The passage keeps distinct questions close enough that a reader can easily confuse them:

- **Physical action:** Who actually opened the gate? The final text does not say.
- **Authorization:** Who requested its opening? “She did” and “We did” offer competing accounts. Mara’s answer asserts shared involvement, but it remains testimony, not independent confirmation.
- **Warning:** Who knew the lower houses were at risk, and who could or should have warned them? The keeper’s question redirects attention toward this possible failure without establishing its circumstances. [B09-S2]

The keeper asks who *asked* for the gate, not who turned the wheel, and writes down neither answer. The scene therefore produces no official resolution of even the authorization dispute. Meanwhile, the narrator finds Mara’s hand easier to examine than the path toward the houses. That preference suggests an evasion of consequences: looking for a culprit can displace looking toward those who may have been harmed. It does not prove that a timely warning was possible.

## What the clearer notice leaves behind

“No one opens this gate alone” can function as an operating instruction, but its wording also allows a broader demand: opening the gate involves shared authorization, vigilance, or responsibility. “Two witnesses required” supplies a countable procedure. Witnessing an action, however, need not mean authorizing it, helping to assess its consequences, or ensuring that houses have been warned. The new wording can therefore make compliance clearer while narrowing the older notice’s moral reach. The passage does not show whether it improves actual safety. [B09-S3]

The narrator’s later position as keeper joins personal memory to institutional authority. Public praise rewards the revised notice; the old board remains privately preserved, turned away, and imagined in Mara’s voice. That combination suggests an unresolved demand beneath the successful clarification. Keeping the board could express attachment or guilt, but neither interpretation is a factual confession of a particular act.

The discarded sentence “Mara turned the wheel” shows that an earlier version resolved physical agency. Its omission matters precisely because that resolution is unavailable in the final text. The jacket’s “confession of a coward” is a later marketing interpretation, not external narrative evidence. Likewise, calling a red palm a universal code for guilt ignores the passage’s explicit alternative explanation. [B09-S4]

A leading reading is therefore that the narrator’s attempt to make Mara solely responsible exposes a possible shared failure instead. What resists a final verdict is specific: we still lack confirmation of the operator, the request, the brother’s fate, and the timing and possibility of warning. The uncertainty concerns how responsibility should be assigned as well as what happened.
