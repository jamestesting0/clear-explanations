The strongest surviving evidence places “night names” within trade and the effort to connect traders with official household records. Deliberate resistance remains a plausible use, but it is less securely established as the original or chief purpose. The archive supports an unevenly weighted account of several functions, not a verdict about what all residents intended.

## The strongest evidence concerns practical use and official recording

The harbor register shows that short names were added beside 19 of 62 surviving trader entries. Thirteen of those 19 traders operated boats after dusk, linking the recorded practice with nighttime trade. The other six lack recorded boat occupations; that is not evidence that they never worked on boats. Because the register concerns taxable movements, its entries show what officials recorded, not the full range of names people used privately. [B05-S1]

The marginal note suggests a distinction between a short name used around the tides and a household identity answerable for tax. However, the annotations are undated, and the note may not be by the person who added the names. We cannot confidently combine them into one dated administrative policy. Nor does official recording establish that officials invented the names. [B05-S1]

The letter dated year 44 provides the clearest statement of a practical aim: four traders wanted faster clearance and asked the clerk to keep both names. Its account describes a mismatch between what inspectors requested and what boatmen supplied. Three signatories match annotated register entries, strengthening the connection between the two documents and particular traders. It does not establish when those annotations were made, and the fourth trader's missing entry cannot be treated as evidence of an exception to the practice. [B05-S2]

The letter supports the petitioners' stated wish to reconcile names for administrative purposes. It does not directly show that night names were originally created to make trading easier: in the situation described, the mismatch was slowing trade. Its survival through an official copy also matters. Confrontational wording could have been softened, as one historian proposes, but no alternative version establishes that this happened. A possible alteration cannot be used as evidence for the missing words. [B05-S2]

## Resistance is suggested by a later, less secure account

The former watch officer's memoir describes night names as frustrating arrest and making official ledgers objects of mockery. If accurate, it would support a use that obstructed authority and, at least among some people, a defiant attitude. But the memoir was written in year 79 about year 43, a gap of 36 years. The author's admitted confusion about flood seasons raises a specific concern about remembered chronology. Its sale during a campaign against a new tax authority also gives reason to consider how the past was selected and presented for a later audience. Neither issue automatically makes the account false. [B05-S3]

The verified gate descriptions establish accuracy about those geographical details. They do not independently confirm the naming story or traders' motives. Corroboration is strongest when it checks the claim under dispute; accuracy elsewhere cannot do that work. [B05-S3]

The song offers still less direct evidence about a historical policy or widespread intention. Its language is compatible with retaining more than one identity, but poetry cannot simply be read as a register of actual behavior. The year 88 date belongs to the surviving copy, not necessarily the composition. The collector's resistance heading is a later interpretation, weakened further by the inclusion of unrelated wedding verses. The year 112 catalog's claim that citizens abandoned household identities goes beyond the fragment, which does not say one name replaced the other. [B05-S4]

## Functions, intentions, and later meanings can differ

A name could work conveniently among boatmen while causing difficulty when an inspector tried to connect it to a taxable household. That difficulty would be an effect of the practice; it would not by itself prove that evading officials was why the name was adopted. Conversely, a request to record both names shows the signatories' practical goal at that moment, without ruling out political intentions among other users—or other motives among the same users.

The best-supported synthesis is therefore that some traders used alternative names, officials encountered problems connecting those names with household identities, and at least four traders sought a practical arrangement that retained both. Official annotations show some incorporation into records, although their timing and relationship to the petition remain uncertain. Later sources gave the practice an explicit association with conflict over authority. Earlier practical uses and later political meanings could coexist; one need not erase the other. [B05-S1–S4]

One live alternative is that some night names were deliberately used to obscure household connections and resist enforcement from an early stage. The memoir is consistent with that possibility, and practical petitions would not exclude it. But making resistance the chief original purpose would require stronger contemporary evidence of intent. The unverified suggestion that the official copy softened the letter cannot supply that evidence.

## What the museum should leave open

The 19 annotations among 62 surviving entries describe this incomplete register, not the prevalence of night names among all traders or residents. Missing pages, selective recording, and the archive's focus on taxable activity prevent that extrapolation. The register's year 41 date does not date later annotations; the letter and memoir do not establish the practice's origin; and the song's copy date cannot establish when it was composed. [B05-S1–S4]

Finally, the documents do not establish one shared motive, an organized movement, a formal town-wide naming policy, or widespread abandonment of household identities. A museum label can confidently present trade and administrative reconciliation as the best-attested setting, identify resistance as a plausible but less securely documented use, and show how later writers made the practice politically meaningful without claiming that later interpretation settles its beginnings.
