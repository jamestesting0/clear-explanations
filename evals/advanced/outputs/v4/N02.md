You multiply by each group's fraction because you want **the average remaining uncertainty for an example**. A randomly chosen example is more likely to come from a large group, so that group's entropy should contribute more.

Entropy measures uncertainty about an example's label. Suppose a split divides 100 examples into two groups:

- **Group A: 90 examples**, all with the same label. Its entropy is **0 bits**: once you know the group, the label is certain.
- **Group B: 10 examples**, evenly divided between two labels. Its entropy is **1 bit**: either label is equally likely.

A randomly chosen example has a 90% chance of belonging to A and a 10% chance of belonging to B. The average remaining entropy is therefore:

\[
\frac{90}{100}\times 0 + \frac{10}{100}\times 1
= 0.1\text{ bits per example}.
\]

Simply averaging the two entropies would give \((0+1)/2=0.5\). That gives the ten-example group as much influence as the ninety-example group. Weighting by group size makes each **example** count equally.

For a decision-tree split, use fractions of the examples reaching that split, which may be only part of the full dataset.
