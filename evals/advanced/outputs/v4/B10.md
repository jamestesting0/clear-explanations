Both presentations can contain correct percentages while drawing incorrect conclusions. Success rose within each recorded difficulty group, but the accepted workload shifted strongly toward Hard requests, whose success rate remained much lower. That shift lowered the overall rate. These comparisons describe the two periods; they do not isolate the tool’s effect or establish fairness between districts.

## The arithmetic and the changing mix

| Accepted requests | Baseline | Tool period | Change in success rate |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Easy | 90 / 100 = 90% | 19 / 20 = 95% | +5 percentage points |
| Hard | 20 / 100 = 20% | 60 / 200 = 30% | +10 percentage points |
| All | 110 / 200 = 55% | 79 / 220 ≈ 35.9% | −19.1 percentage points |

These denominators contain only accepted requests, all with complete seven-day outcomes. [B10-S1]

The baseline mix was half Easy and half Hard. In the tool period, only 20 / 220 ≈ 9.1% were Easy, while 90.9% were Hard. The overall rate is an average weighted by these shares. Although Hard performance improved to 30%, that rate remained far below Easy performance at 95%. Giving Hard requests much more weight pulled down the combined result.

This reversal between within-group and pooled comparisons is often called **Simpson’s paradox**. There is no arithmetic contradiction: the comparisons use different mixes. The first presentation is wrong that subgroup improvements force the overall rate upward. The second is wrong that an overall decline proves harm to every type of customer; both reported subgroup rates increased, and neither those increases nor the pooled decline establish individual causal effects. [B10-S3]

## What reweighting answers

Applying the tool-period rates to the baseline’s fifty-fifty mix gives:

\[
0.5(95\%)+0.5(30\%)=62.5\%.
\]

That is **7.5 percentage points above** the baseline’s 55%. It answers: “What weighted rate results if we hold the Easy/Hard shares at their baseline values and use the observed tool-period rates?” This is **descriptive standardization**: making the category weights comparable.

It does not establish that the tool caused a 7.5-point benefit. Training changed at the same time; the new contract changed acceptance; managers made undocumented overrides; and there was no concurrent untreated comparison or random assignment. Even within Hard, the accepted cases could have different unrecorded severity. Fixed category definitions prevent relabeling from explaining the result, but they do not make the cases otherwise equivalent. The 62.5% figure is also based on observed sample rates, including only 20 Easy requests in the tool period. [B10-S1–S3]

## Customer outcomes and district fairness remain separate questions

The reported metric asks whether an **accepted** request met a seven-day deadline. A later satisfactory repair still fails that metric. There were 110 recorded timely completions in the baseline period and 79 in the tool period; these are counts, not necessarily comparable rates per unit of calendar time. They do not count all eventual successful repairs. [B10-S1]

We cannot determine outcomes for incoming customers as a whole because rejected and abandoned requests were not retained. Nor can we infer how much unmet demand existed. Waiting before acceptance, eventual repairs, and access to the promise can change without being captured by the accepted-request success percentage. [B10-S2, B10-S4]

Nothing supplied establishes an East–West difference. Even equal success rates among accepted requests would not prove comparable access: selection can leave similar-looking accepted groups after very different admission decisions. The district field makes a future analysis possible; it supplies no missing cross-tabulation here. [B10-S4]

## A defensible evaluation

First, record **every incoming request**, including its district, predecision repair characteristics, tool recommendation, acceptance or rejection, override reason, abandonment, and relevant timestamps. Follow outcomes long enough to distinguish seven-day completion from later successful repair. Record unknown outcomes explicitly rather than treating missing follow-up as success.

Second, make the district comparison explicit. Agree in advance which predecision features—such as repair type, severity, and urgency—make requests comparable. Within those groups, compare East and West on:

- The fraction of incoming requests accepted for the promise and the wait before acceptance.
- Seven-day completion among accepted requests.
- Timely completion among all incoming requests, with subsequent outcomes tracked for those rejected or abandoned.

Standardize each district to the same pooled incoming-request mix, report the district differences and their uncertainty, and retain the within-group results so an aggregate does not hide a particular access problem. For the access policy, use equal acceptance probabilities for comparable requests as the stated target; any permitted operational difference should be specified before judging results. Completion parity should be assessed alongside that access target, not substituted for it.

Finally, estimate the tool’s effect prospectively using randomized tool availability across suitable teams or periods, with the same contract and training conditions and a recorded decision policy. Compare all incoming requests in the assigned groups, so changes in acceptance remain part of the measured effect. If randomization is impractical, a concurrent comparison with detailed case information would improve the evidence, while leaving unmeasured differences as a limitation. This would allow the network to evaluate the tool’s contribution separately from workload composition and to judge customer access alongside completion.
