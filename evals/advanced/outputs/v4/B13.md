Rush earns more per job, but Standard earns more per scarce workshop hour. Under the stated weekly model, the best use of the existing workshop is **100 Standard jobs and 10 Rush jobs**. Buying the additional block then increases the best achievable weekly profit by **750 currency units**.

## What the proposed schedule changes

Contribution is sales revenue minus variable materials cost. Profit then subtracts the weekly fixed expenses of 2,000. Those fixed expenses do not change with the mix inside the existing 120 hours. [B13-S1]

| Weekly measure | Baseline: 100 Standard, 0 Rush | Proposed: 80 Standard, 20 Rush | Change |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Workshop hours used | 100 | 120 | +20 |
| Revenue | 10,000 | 11,600 | +1,600 |
| Variable materials | 3,000 | 3,400 | +400 |
| Contribution | 7,000 | 8,200 | +1,200 |
| Profit | 5,000 | 6,200 | +1,200 |

The revenue increase is **16% of baseline revenue**, whereas the profit increase is **24% of baseline profit**. Contribution rises by approximately 17.1% of baseline contribution. The amounts and denominators differ: the extra 1,600 of revenue produces only 1,200 of extra profit because materials cost rises by 400. Contribution and profit increase by the same amount here because fixed expenses stay unchanged. [Calculations from B13-S1–S2]

## Why contribution per hour and demand both matter

| Product | Contribution per job | Hours per job | Contribution per workshop hour |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Standard | 100 − 30 = 70 | 1 | 70 |
| Rush | 180 − 50 = 130 | 2 | 65 |

When both products have available customers, the two hours used by one Rush job could instead earn 140 from two Standard jobs. Choosing Rush in that situation gives up 10 of contribution. The 130-per-job comparison overlooks this use of scarce time. [B13-S1]

But the shop cannot sell 120 Standard repairs: demand stops at 100. After serving those 100 jobs, it has 20 hours left and can serve 10 Rush jobs without displacing any Standard work. This gives:

- Revenue: 10,000 + 1,800 = **11,800**.
- Contribution: 7,000 + 1,300 = **8,300**.
- Profit: 8,300 − 2,000 = **6,300**.

This mix respects both demand limits and uses all 120 hours. Removing a Rush job would leave time that cannot be sold to additional Standard customers; adding a Rush job would require sacrificing two Standard jobs and losing 10 of contribution. It therefore maximizes profit under the supplied assumptions. [Calculation from B13-S1]

The proposed schedule earns 100 less than this optimum: it replaces 20 Standard jobs with 10 additional Rush jobs. A Rush-first policy would sell the maximum 30 Rush jobs and then 60 Standard jobs, earning only 6,100 profit. Filling all capacity with Rush alone is infeasible because 60 Rush jobs would exceed its demand ceiling of 30.

## Evaluate the capacity offer against the best existing mix

Starting from 100 Standard and 10 Rush, Standard demand is exhausted, but demand remains for another 20 Rush jobs. The additional 30 hours can therefore be fully used for 15 more Rush jobs. The best expanded mix is **100 Standard and 25 Rush**. [B13-S1, B13-S3]

The additional jobs generate 2,700 revenue and cost 750 in materials, leaving 1,950 contribution. Subtracting the indivisible block fee gives:

**Additional profit = 2,700 − 750 − 1,200 = 750.**

Total weekly profit becomes 10,250 contribution − 2,000 existing fixed expenses − 1,200 block fee = **7,050**, compared with 6,300 without the block. The offer is worthwhile in this model because sufficient demand exists and the added contribution exceeds the fee. Extra revenue alone would not establish that. Serving all stated demand would require 160 hours, so five Rush jobs remain unserved even after expansion.

## What these results establish

The demand limits support choosing a weekly mix and evaluating this specific capacity offer **at the posted prices**, treating the survey quantities as exact as instructed. They do not establish the best prices: there is no information about how quantities would respond to a price change. Nor do they establish future demand or real payment behavior; cancellations and willingness to complete purchases remain untested outside the exercise. [B13-S4]

The profit calculation also does not measure customer welfare or social benefit. That would require evidence about how much customers value the repairs, benefits beyond what they pay, and effects on others, including other shops. Rush's higher price supplies none of those missing comparisons and cannot establish that it is “socially optimal.” [B13-S4]

Within the fixed assumptions, allowing Rush and optimizing the existing workshop raises maximum profit from 5,000 to 6,300. That is a **conditional model comparison**. The baseline and proposed schedule do not independently establish the real-world causal effect of introducing Rush: they change the mix and use previously idle capacity, without providing an observed comparison that isolates introduction from other changes in conditions.
