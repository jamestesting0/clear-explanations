The low reported latency and the large backlog describe different populations. The latency histogram describes jobs that finished; the unfinished-job gauge also includes jobs that remained stuck. The supplied figures do **not** justify doubling the workers: dependency blocking is a credible cause, and worker capacity has not been established as the limiting factor.

## What the counts establish

The flow balance is exact:

\[
120\text{ initially unfinished}+600\text{ admitted}-540\text{ completed}
=180\text{ finally unfinished}.
\]

Over 600 seconds, the admission rate was **1 job/second**, the completion rate was **0.9 jobs/second**, and unfinished work increased by 60 jobs, averaging **0.1 job/second**. Calling 540 / 600 the arrival rate confuses completions with admissions. The measured mean of 150 unfinished jobs includes waiting, executing, and dependency-blocked jobs; it does not mean that 150 workers were occupied. [B08-S1, B08-S3]

## Why the latency statistics can stay low

The mean of 20 seconds and the ninety-fifth percentile of 25 seconds concern the **540 completed jobs**. Their durations run from original admission to completion, even where admission preceded this window. Roughly 95 percent of that completed-job sample finished within 25 seconds, subject to the percentile convention. This says neither that 95 percent of the 600 new admissions finished that quickly nor that 95 percent of all jobs present did. The 180 unfinished jobs are absent from the histogram. [B08-S2]

If one class continues normally while another waits indefinitely for a dependency, the normal jobs keep supplying short completed durations. The stalled jobs keep increasing the unfinished count and their own ages without contributing a completed duration. That mechanism fits the reported dependency interruption, although the missing class labels prevent us from identifying every stuck job. [B08-S3]

## Why the average-work relationship does not resolve this window

The relationship usually called Little’s law connects long-run average jobs present, admission rate, and total time per job for the **same system and population**, under conditions where those averages exist and work does not accumulate without bound. It does not require all jobs to behave alike, but applying it to short-window measurements requires care about jobs crossing the window boundaries.

Here, the gauge counts each job only for the portion of its lifetime inside the ten-minute window. The histogram instead counts the full lifetimes of completers and none of the unfinished lifetimes. Those are different accounting rules. Some jobs were already present at the start; others remain at the end. Consequently, multiplying 1 job/second by the completers’ 20-second mean is not a valid prediction of this window’s mean unfinished count. Likewise, 150 / 1 = 150 seconds is not a directly measured mean lifetime of the admitted jobs.

The summaries also impose a useful quantitative constraint; they do not permit just any explanation:

- The gauge represents 150 × 600 = **90,000 job-seconds** spent unfinished inside the window.
- All completed jobs’ full lifetimes together total 540 × 20 = **10,800 job-seconds**. Their contribution inside the window cannot exceed that total.
- Therefore, the 180 jobs unfinished at the end contributed **at least 79,200 job-seconds**, averaging at least **440 seconds each inside this window**.

Thus the unfinished jobs must account for most of the measured occupancy. They cannot all have arrived near the end. Any time completers spent before the window makes that lower bound larger. This explains how the instruments can agree without proving that either instrument is correct in every detail. [B08-S1–S2]

## What a scaling decision needs

Forty configured workers and 22 percent average CPU utilization do not establish a worker shortage. They also do not rule out a shortage affecting a particular worker pool or job class. Because execution slots can be released during external waits, unfinished-job count cannot substitute for occupied-slot count. Adding workers will not itself make an unresponsive dependency respond and could increase pressure on it. [B08-S3]

The most useful additional measurements are:

1. **Where jobs are waiting, split by class:** counts and ages for jobs awaiting a worker, executing, and awaiting the dependency, including the oldest unfinished jobs. Follow admission cohorts through completion so stuck jobs remain visible.
2. **Actual worker capacity:** occupied execution slots, runnable jobs waiting for slots, per-pool utilization, and execution time. This distinguishes unavailable workers from released slots and external waiting.
3. **Dependency behavior:** latency, outstanding requests, timeouts, errors, and rate limits, linked to job class. Compare these with changes in the incoming and unfinished workload mix.

Use that evidence to decide whether more workers can increase useful completions; a limited scaling trial can then test the proposed bottleneck.

Finally, 180 / 0.9 = **200 seconds** is a conditional drain estimate after admissions stop, assuming the same completion rate can be sustained until every remaining job finishes. It is not an exact forecast: the remaining jobs may be disproportionately blocked, and throughput may fall as the queue drains. The quarterly uptime statement supplies no missing evidence about this backlog or completion-time distribution. [B08-S4]
