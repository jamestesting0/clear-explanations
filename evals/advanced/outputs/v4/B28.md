The experiment provides a randomized comparison of **assignment to training when exactly half of each team is assigned**. It does not establish what would happen if the company trained everyone rather than nobody, and it does not isolate an employee's own training effect while keeping every teammate's assignment unchanged.

## What the observed difference means

Assigned employees made 160 errors in 2,000 checks, or 8%; unassigned employees made 200 errors in 2,000 checks, or 10%. The assigned group's error rate was **2 percentage points lower**, equivalent to 20% fewer errors relative to the unassigned group's 10% rate (B28-S1).

That is an observed estimate, not proof that the underlying causal effect equals exactly two percentage points. Employees were the units assigned, and each completed repeated checks. The 4,000 checks should not be treated as 4,000 independently randomized participants. The supplied totals contain no individual outcome variation or uncertainty estimates with which to assess the precision of the comparison.

Assignment also differs from what people actually received. At least six unassigned employees received explanations of the checklist; how much they learned is unknown. The randomized comparison remains a comparison by original assignment, including sharing that occurred under that arrangement. Regrouping employees according to whether they actually obtained checklist information would not preserve randomization: access to colleagues' explanations was not randomly assigned (B28-S2).

## Why sharing changes the causal question

A colleague's training can affect someone else's outcome—a spillover. Sharing might help, while training time might reduce the help colleagues otherwise provide. Neither the overall size nor the direction of these effects is established.

The fixed team allocation creates an additional, precise distinction:

| Employee's own assignment | Other assigned teammates |
| --- | ---: |
| Assigned | 4 |
| Unassigned | 5 |

Thus the randomized contrast averages outcomes under “assigned, with four assigned teammates” versus “unassigned, with five assigned teammates,” over the teammate identities produced by randomization. Those identities may matter as well as the count. This is a meaningful causal contrast under the actual allocation design. It combines a difference in one's own assignment with a difference in the distribution of peer assignments; the design cannot change one while keeping the other fixed.

A company-wide rollout asks a different question. In these ten-person teams, universal training would give each employee training plus nine assigned teammates; no training would give neither the employee nor any teammate an assignment. Neither configuration occurred in the randomized teams. Effects under those configurations cannot be recovered simply by extending the observed two-point difference.

Sharing therefore does not make randomization meaningless. Nor must it make the estimate too small: beneficial sharing, competition for time, and behavioral responses could affect the contrast differently. The packet supplies no basis for choosing a direction.

## Why the other comparisons do not fill the gap

The historical 20% rate came from a previous task version. Comparing it with the assigned employees' current 8% mixes training assignment with changes in tasks and time, as well as possible effects of working in a partially trained team. The 12-percentage-point decline cannot be attributed wholly to training (B28-S3).

The distant teams' decline from 18% to 16% also lacks randomized assignment to the experimental conditions. Their different tasks and baseline experience prevent their trend from automatically serving as the experimental teams' untreated trend. Neither comparison supplies a randomized universal-training-versus-no-training contrast; subtracting one trend from another would require additional comparability assumptions.

## A study matched to the rollout decision

Randomly assigning many teams to zero, partial, or full training would create the missing variation in team policy. With comparable assessments and spillovers across teams addressed, the full-versus-zero comparison could estimate the overall effect of rollout, including the changes in colleagues' behavior that rollout causes.

Within partially trained teams, random individual assignment would support further assignment comparisons. Comparing unassigned employees in partially trained teams with employees in zero-training teams could estimate effects of exposure to trained colleagues. However, a fixed-size partial allocation still couples own assignment with the number of assigned peers. Isolating an own-training effect at unchanged peer exposure would require assignment variation that permits both own-assignment states at that exposure, together with a clear account of which peer assignments matter.

Forbidding communication would instead study training under a different workplace condition. It might reduce information sharing, but would not automatically remove every indirect effect, such as competition for time. Its findings would not directly answer the proposed rollout question for a workplace where employees normally collaborate (B28-S4).
