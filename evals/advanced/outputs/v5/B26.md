**Under the stipulated accounting, total social surplus is 900 in the baseline, 700 under Tax, and 920 under Clean.** Clean has the highest total, although its gains and costs fall on different parties. Tax collection and gross sales measure money flows; neither is itself an additional benefit to the island.

## Accounting for each party

Every scenario produces total purchaser benefit of \(100\times22=2{,}200\). This benefit comes from the supplied valuations, not from the selling price. [B26-S1]

- **Buyer surplus** is purchaser benefit minus buyers' total payments.
- **Producer contribution before common fixed costs** is gross sales minus tax remittances and production resource costs. It is not gross sales or a claim about final profit after those fixed costs.
- **Council net receipts or costs** are tax collections minus the relevant administration or monitoring costs.
- **Nuisance damage** is a loss to other residents, even though no payment compensates them.

Using the supplied outcomes gives the following period totals, in currency units. [B26-S1, B26-S2, B26-S3]

| Measure | Baseline | Tax | Clean |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Purchaser benefit | 2,200 | 2,200 | 2,200 |
| Buyer payments / producer gross sales | 1,500 | 2,000 | 1,700 |
| Buyer surplus | 700 | 200 | 500 |
| Producer tax remittance | 0 | 500 | 0 |
| Production resource cost | 1,000 | 1,000 | 1,200 |
| Producer contribution | 500 | 500 | 500 |
| Council net receipts or costs | 0 | 300 | −80 |
| Nuisance damage | 300 | 300 | 0 |
| **Total social surplus** | **900** | **700** | **920** |

For example, the Tax producer's contribution is \(2{,}000-500-1{,}000=500\). Its extra 500 in gross sales is entirely remitted, so its contribution does not rise. The council retains \(500-200=300\) after administration. Under Clean, the council's 80 monitoring cost counts even though it is paid from existing funds. [B26-S2, B26-S3]

## Why the two accounting methods agree

Adding the parties' positions gives

\[
\text{social surplus}=\text{buyer surplus}+\text{producer contribution}
+\text{council net receipts}-\text{nuisance damage}.
\]

Product payments cancel between buyers and producers. Taxes cancel between producers and the council. Counting tax revenue again as an extra social gain would count the same transfer twice. What remains is benefit minus real costs. [B26-S1]

| Scenario | Direct calculation | Result |
| --- | --- | ---: |
| Baseline | 2,200 − 1,000 production − 300 nuisance | 900 |
| Tax | 2,200 − 1,000 production − 300 nuisance − 200 administration | 700 |
| Clean | 2,200 − 1,200 production − 0 nuisance − 80 monitoring | 920 |

Tax lowers total surplus by **200 relative to baseline** because it adds administration while leaving quantity, production costs, and nuisance unchanged. Its 500 revenue redistributes money; the stipulated outcome supplies no reduction in damage and no additional benefit from future spending. [B26-S2]

Clean raises total surplus by **20 relative to baseline**: avoiding 300 of nuisance outweighs 200 of additional production resources and 80 of monitoring. Buyers nevertheless lose 200 of surplus, producers' contribution stays unchanged, and the council bears 80. Thus higher buyer payments alone do not establish a social loss. Clean exceeds Tax by **220** in this model. [B26-S1, B26-S2, B26-S3]

## What the ranking does not establish

Equal weighting is a value judgment: one person's valued gain can offset another's equal valued loss, without extra weight for who bears the loss or their ability to afford it. A higher total therefore does not establish that everyone benefits or that the distribution is acceptable. [B26-S1]

These scenarios do not identify an optimal future tax rate. They supply no responses of output, production methods, prices, or costs to alternative rates, and do not establish that an unconstrained market would reproduce these outcomes. Industrial heritage, innovation, employment, and future savings add nothing to this calculation without additional specified benefits or costs. [B26-S2, B26-S3, B26-S4]
