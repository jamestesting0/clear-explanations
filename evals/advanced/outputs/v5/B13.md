The best use of the existing workshop is **100 Standard repairs and 10 Rush repairs**, producing a weekly profit of **6,300 currency units**. Rush earns more per job, but Standard earns more per scarce workshop hour. Demand limits then determine how much of each the shop can actually sell. [B13-S1]

## What the proposed schedule changes

Contribution means sales revenue minus variable materials costs. Profit then subtracts the fixed weekly expenses of 2,000. Because those fixed expenses do not change with the mix, an increase in contribution produces the same absolute increase in profit. [B13-S1]

| Weekly measure | Baseline: 100 Standard, 0 Rush | Proposed: 80 Standard, 20 Rush | Change |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Workshop hours used | 100 | 120 | +20 |
| Revenue | 10,000 | 11,600 | +1,600 |
| Variable materials costs | 3,000 | 3,400 | +400 |
| Contribution | 7,000 | 8,200 | +1,200 |
| Fixed expenses | 2,000 | 2,000 | 0 |
| Profit | 5,000 | 6,200 | +1,200 |

These figures follow from the supplied prices, costs, and schedules. [B13-S1; B13-S2] For example, proposed revenue is `80 × 100 + 20 × 180 = 11,600`, while materials cost `80 × 30 + 20 × 50 = 3,400`.

The revenue increase is therefore **1,600, or 16% of baseline revenue**. The profit increase is **1,200, or 24% of baseline profit**. They differ both in amount, because materials costs rise, and in percentage, because their starting values differ. Contribution rises about 17.1% relative to its own baseline of 7,000.

## Why the highest contribution per job is not the best priority

| Product | Contribution per job | Hours per job | Contribution per hour |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Standard | 100 − 30 = 70 | 1 | 70 |
| Rush | 180 − 50 = 130 | 2 | 65 |

When Standard demand remains available, two hours can earn 140 from two Standard jobs, versus 130 from one Rush job. Giving those hours to Rush sacrifices 10 of contribution. [B13-S1]

But the shop cannot sell 120 Standard repairs: demand stops at 100. After accepting those 100 jobs, it has 20 hours left, enough for 10 Rush jobs. This uses all 120 hours and respects both demand ceilings. Its contribution is `100 × 70 + 10 × 130 = 8,300`, giving profit of **6,300**. [B13-S1]

This also establishes optimality: each hour can contribute at most 70, and Standard can occupy only 100 hours; the remaining 20 earn 65 each through Rush. The proposed mix earns 100 less because it replaces 20 Standard jobs with 10 additional Rush jobs. Filling all capacity with Rush is infeasible: its 30-job demand ceiling uses only 60 hours. Even accepting all 30 Rush jobs first and then 60 Standard jobs yields only 6,100 profit.

## Should the shop buy the extra 30 hours?

**Yes, under this model: the block raises optimized weekly profit by 750.** With 150 hours, the best mix becomes 100 Standard and 25 Rush. Standard demand is already exhausted, while the remaining Rush demand can use the entire block. [B13-S1; B13-S3]

Compared with the best existing-capacity mix, the block permits 15 extra Rush jobs:

- Extra revenue: `15 × 180 = 2,700`.
- Extra materials costs: `15 × 50 = 750`.
- Extra contribution: `2,700 − 750 = 1,950`.
- Extra profit after the block fee: `1,950 − 1,200 = 750`.

Total profit becomes `10,250 − 2,000 − 1,200 = 7,050`. Extra revenue alone would not justify the purchase: the additional contribution must cover the full block fee. Here it does, with sufficient demand to fill the hours; that is a result of these particular assumptions, not a general property of added capacity.

## What these results establish

The calculations optimize weekly profit **at the supplied prices, costs, capacity choices, and exact demand ceilings**. They do not identify better prices: that would require knowing how demand changes with price. The reservation survey also cannot establish future paid demand without checking follow-through and cancellations. [B13-S4]

A higher Rush price does not establish greater customer welfare or social optimality. The packet gives neither customers' willingness to pay above the posted prices nor effects on others, including other shops. Profit accounting cannot fill those gaps. [B13-S4]

Finally, the 1,300 profit gain from the baseline to the best existing-capacity mix is a conditional model comparison. It does not independently establish the real-world causal effect of introducing Rush: unchanged Standard demand is an assumption here, and the packet supplies no controlled comparison separating introduction of Rush from other changes.
