The museum has credible contemporary evidence that a listener at the south quay heard no answer from the North Bell. It has weaker evidence that the bell did not ring anywhere, and insufficient evidence to identify a mechanical failure. These are three different claims, requiring different observations.

### What the diary establishes

**Not hearing a bell** describes a listener's experience. **A bell not ringing** describes what happened at the bell. **A mechanism being unable to ring** identifies a reason it could not sound, even if someone tried to operate it.

The diary reports the first: no northern answer reached the diarist's quay. Its placement among dated entries supports its contemporary timing. Hearing the South Bell shows that the writer heard that bell, but does not establish that the North Bell would normally have been audible from the same location. A warehouse ridge intervened, and the packet leaves normal audibility and the wind's effect unresolved. The diarist did not inspect the North Bell. [B22-S1]

Even if the North Bell did not ring, mechanical failure would not automatically follow. An operable bell might not have been rung. The packet does not establish whether anyone attempted to ring it.

### Three texts are not necessarily three independent witnesses

The year-45 chronicle lists the harbor diary among its sources. Its stronger claim that the bell was silent could therefore derive from the diary's narrower listening report, although the missing individual footnotes prevent tracing that sentence with certainty. [B22-S2]

The year-73 school history has especially strong signs of dependence on the chronicle: it repeats an unusual phrase, reproduces a mistaken gate spelling, and names the chronicle in its bibliography. Its agreement consequently cannot simply be counted as another independent observation of the storm. The packet supports possible or likely inheritance, not a complete reconstruction of every sentence's origin. [B22-S2]

That dependence does not make either book worthless. They show how these later writers represented the storm and used the bell's silence as a symbol of absent leadership. That is evidence about later historical storytelling and public memory, rather than independent proof of the original mechanism's condition. Their dates alone neither disqualify them nor establish their accuracy. Their shared symbolism also does not prove that leadership was actually absent. [B22-S2]

### Explanations that remain open

**1. The North Bell did not ring because something prevented its operation.** The diary's non-hearing and the later silence narrative fit this explanation. The repair invoice supplies a possible connection to the rope, but only records replacement two months later. The rope might have failed during the storm, failed afterward, or been replaced preventively. It cannot establish a storm-night rope failure or another unique mechanical cause. [B22-S1–S3]

**2. The bell rang, but its sound did not reach the south-quay listener.** The intervening ridge and recorded strong wind make this a live alternative. The reconstructed-bell test demonstrates that non-hearing in strong wind is physically conceivable in this setting. However, differences in height and material prevent treating that test as an exact reconstruction, and it provides no calibrated probability for year 27. This explanation fits the diary directly; the later books could have converted a report of non-hearing into a broader claim of silence. That last step remains an inference about their relationship. [B22-S1–S3]

**3. The bell remained usable but was not rung.** This also separates silence from mechanical inability. There is no positive evidence here identifying an operational decision or omission, so it should remain a possibility rather than become a new asserted story.

The weather log corroborates the storm, not the bell's behavior. The festival's popularity contributes nothing to choosing among these explanations. [B22-S3; B22-S4]

### What would sharpen the conclusion

A securely dated, independent bellkeeper's record describing a storm-night attempt to ring the bell and the mechanism's observed condition would be highly informative. An account of successful ringing from someone beside the North Bell would favor the inaudibility explanation; an account of an attempted pull obstructed by a contemporaneously observed broken rope would favor mechanical failure. Either would address a gap that the later repair invoice leaves open.

The proposed 97% figure has no justified foundation: the assumed witness accuracies, dependence model, starting probability, and event-specific evidence probabilities are missing. Counting likely related narratives as independent confirmations would exaggerate their support. [B22-S4]

Suitable museum wording is: **“A diary from the storm night records that no answer from the North Bell was heard at the south quay. Later histories describe the bell as silent, but may inherit an earlier account. Whether it rang, and why it might not have rung, remains unresolved.”**
