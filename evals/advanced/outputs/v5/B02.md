The reports address different questions, so their results need not contradict one another. Together they suggest that Luma can reduce heat entering through a roof under some conditions, but they do **not establish that it improves comfort in the town's actual housing**, especially during humid evenings in top-floor apartments.

### What was assigned or selected, and what was measured?

| Report | How the comparison was formed | What was measured | What it can support |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Randomized pilot | Twenty buildings were assigned to coatings by chance | Average afternoon indoor temperature; a comfort questionnaire with incomplete responses | The strongest available comparison for attributing an afternoon temperature difference to Luma |
| Contractor comparison | Owners chose coating, other improvements, and survey participation | Reported afternoons of discomfort | An association between the voluntarily coated group and fewer reported discomfort afternoons |
| Chamber experiment | Six paired panels were compared under controlled conditions | Inward heat flow | A physical effect under the tested roof material and chamber conditions |

These differences matter because heat flow, room temperature, and residents' comfort are related but distinct outcomes. A percentage change in one is not automatically the same percentage change in another. [B02-S1–S3]

### The pilot is uncertain, not a demonstration of no effect

The pilot's estimate was **0.6 temperature units cooler** with Luma. Its cluster-adjusted uncertainty interval ran from 1.4 units cooler to 0.2 units warmer. Here, “cluster-adjusted” means the analysis accounts for apartments belonging to the same building and sharing conditions. The interval includes no difference, but also includes potentially useful cooling; crossing zero does not establish that the true effect is zero. [B02-S1]

The coating was assigned to 20 buildings, not independently to 740 apartments. Apartments under the same roof share exposure and ventilation, so counting all of them as separate experimental assignments exaggerates how much independent information the trial contains. The later analyst's narrower interval therefore does not overturn the building-level analysis. More apartment measurements can improve information about a building, but they do not create additional independently assigned buildings. [B02-S1]

The comfort evidence has a separate problem: questionnaire responses came from 78% of control occupants but only 51% of treated occupants. If respondents differed from nonrespondents in comfort, comparing respondents could give a misleading picture. The direction of that distortion is unknown because reasons for nonresponse are missing. Occupants also knew their coating, which could affect subjective reports. Neither issue proves that the reports are wrong; both limit what can be concluded from them. The packet supplies no comfort scores from which to calculate a reliable benefit. [B02-S1]

### The contractor's larger sample does not isolate Luma

The contractor reports 30% fewer discomfort afternoons in coated buildings. But external shades were installed in 70% of those buildings versus 15% of uncoated buildings. Luma and shade installation therefore travel together in this comparison, and the analysis adjusted only for building age. Insulation, neighborhood, previous discomfort, and who elected to participate after hearing complaints could also explain part of the difference. [B02-S2]

A larger sample can reduce random fluctuation without removing these systematic differences. Calling the pilot underpowered—too small to reliably distinguish some plausible effects—does not make the contractor's estimate causal. Nor can the committee translate the relative 30% difference into an absolute number of afternoons saved without the underlying counts and definitions. [B02-S2]

### The chamber supplies a mechanism, with transfer limits

The 18% reduction in inward heat flow supports a physical benefit in the six paired panels under fixed radiation, equal airflow, dry surfaces, and no internal heat sources. It does not validate the contractor's 30% comfort estimate: it measures a different outcome in a different setting. [B02-S3]

Only one of the town's three common roof materials was tested. The wet-surface estimate was 3%, but absent replication and uncertainty information, its precision and the strength of the difference from the dry result cannot be assessed. A wet surface is also not interchangeable with a humid evening. The available methods and numerical results allow scrutiny; manufacturer funding alone neither establishes nor defeats the findings. The design award supplies no evidence about heat or comfort. [B02-S3; B02-S4]

### What would answer the committee's question?

The main applicability gap is specific: most local complaints concern top-floor apartments during humid evenings, whereas the pilot measured average afternoon temperature and half its buildings were outside town in a drier area. Even a more precise estimate for that pilot would not automatically establish the effect in the housing and conditions the committee cares about. [B02-S1; B02-S4]

The most valuable next study would randomly assign **local buildings** to Luma or the existing coating and follow them over the same summer period. It should include the town's three roof materials and relevant housing types, record other improvements such as shades, and make top-floor comfort during humid evenings a specified outcome before results are examined. Repeated temperature and humidity measurements would connect physical changes to repeated resident comfort assessments. Consistent follow-up of residents and an analysis of missing responses would address the current questionnaire gap. [B02-S1–S4]

The analysis should retain the building as the unit of assignment and explain whether its average represents buildings or apartments, since buildings vary greatly in size. The study needs enough buildings to distinguish a benefit the committee would consider worthwhile, rather than simply a large count of apartments.

A modest overall average could conceal a larger benefit for the hottest apartments—or no benefit for them. That possibility calls for a planned comparison using apartments identified as hot before treatment, not a favorable subgroup selected afterward. The present packet supplies no such result. A local randomized study focused on the relevant time, residents, and roof materials would address both attribution and applicability; merely enlarging the contractor's selected sample would leave those gaps in place.
