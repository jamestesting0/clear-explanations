The archive most strongly supports **a practical naming practice among some traders that officials recorded alongside household identities**. It also preserves a later political interpretation of those names. It does not establish that resistance was their chief purpose, that officials created the practice, or that the same explanation applies to all residents.

### The strongest evidence concerns trade and administration

The harbor register shows that a later hand added night names beside 19 of the 62 surviving household-name entries. Thirteen of those 19 traders operated boats after dusk. That association supports a connection with river work, although the six entries without recorded boat occupations cannot simply be classified as non-boat workers. The marginal instruction links short names to the tide and household names to taxation, suggesting that the two identifiers could serve different practical purposes. [B05-S1]

The register also demonstrates administrative recording: someone made room for both names within a tax-related document. But recording a practice is different from inventing it or making it a formal category throughout the town. The annotations are undated, and the note and names have not been attributed to the same clerk. They therefore cannot be treated as one securely dated naming policy. [B05-S1]

The year 44 merchants' letter gives the clearest evidence of a stated practical intention. Four traders complain that inspectors' requests for household names and boatmen's use of water names delay the barley, then ask the clerk to keep both. Their explicit aim is faster clearance through recognition of both identifiers. Three signatories match annotated register entries, connecting this complaint with some of the people visible there. The fourth's absence from the surviving register proves little because the register is incomplete. [B05-S2]

This makes trading convenience and administrative recognition complementary possibilities: a name useful among boatmen could become an administrative problem when inspectors use a different identifier, and recording both could resolve that problem.

The official-copy problem remains real but limited. A clerk might have softened confrontational language, as the historian proposes. Without an independent version, however, that possibility cannot supply political statements missing from the surviving petition. The letter supports its stated request more strongly than an inferred hidden agenda. [B05-S2]

### Resistance is a supported interpretation, but a weaker account of the original purpose

The watch officer's memoir explicitly presents night names as frustrating official control and associates them with youthful mockery of the ledgers. It therefore supplies evidence that naming could be remembered and represented as resistance. It may also preserve a real recollection of earlier confrontation. [B05-S3]

Several limits prevent treating it as decisive testimony about traders' chief motives in year 43. It was written in year 79, 36 years later; the author acknowledges a memory error elsewhere; and the book was sold during a campaign against a new tax authority. Those circumstances give reason to examine both memory and political presentation. They do not prove fabrication. Accurate descriptions of three gates corroborate his geographical knowledge, not the separate story about naming or what traders intended. [B05-S3]

The song fragment supplies still less direct evidence about historical behavior. Its imagery permits a reading in which different names accompany different settings or aspects of life. Nothing says that one identity replaced the other, and “at home I buried neither” fits continued coexistence better than the later catalog's claim of abandonment. But a poetic speaker is not a survey of actual residents. [B05-S4]

The year 88 date belongs to the surviving copy, not necessarily to the song's composition. The collector's resistance heading is an interpretation, weakened by the inclusion of unrelated wedding verses. The year 112 catalog is evidence of a later interpretation, not contemporary testimony that citizens discarded household identities. [B05-S4]

### Function, intention, and later use can differ

A naming practice can help people identify one another in trade, create difficulty for inspectors, and later become a symbol of resistance. Those functions can coexist without every participant intending all of them.

The letter establishes that its petitioners wanted a practical administrative change. The register shows official accommodation in particular entries. The memoir presents obstruction and mockery. None of these establishes one shared motive across traders, officials, and other residents. Later political uses do not erase an earlier practical role, and an earlier practical role does not rule out contemporary acts of resistance. [B05-S1–S4]

The strongest defensible synthesis for the museum is therefore that **night names served as alternative identifiers in the river-trading world visible in these records; their interaction with household-based taxation created both practical friction and opportunities for political interpretation**. The trade and administrative functions have the strongest direct support. Resistance remains credible as one use or meaning, but the archive does not establish it as the chief origin or purpose.

One live alternative is that deliberate evasion or mockery already accompanied ordinary trading use in the early period, while the official petition emphasized an acceptable practical complaint. The memoir and the letter's description of delays make that possible. They do not establish the suppressed wording or participants' intentions needed to make it the leading conclusion.

The selected archive cannot tell us how common night names were among all residents, when they began, or whether practical, administrative, and resistant uses followed a single sequence. Nineteen annotated entries out of 62 surviving entries describe this incomplete register, not town-wide prevalence. Undated annotations and an undated song composition also prevent a secure chronology of the practice. Claims about a town-wide motive would go beyond the surviving evidence. [B05-S1–S4]
