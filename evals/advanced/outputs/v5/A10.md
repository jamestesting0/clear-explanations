**Both statements about the observed rates are true: the new workflow is 10 percentage points higher within each complexity group, yet 30 percentage points lower overall.** The overall comparison gives much more weight to hard cases for the new workflow. Neither comparison, by itself, establishes what switching workflows would cause.

### Raw and within-group comparisons

The completion rates calculated from [A10-DATA] are:

| Pre-existing complexity | Old workflow | New workflow | New minus old |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Easy | 144 / 180 = **80%** | 18 / 20 = **90%** | **+10 percentage points** |
| Hard | 6 / 20 = **30%** | 72 / 180 = **40%** | **+10 percentage points** |

The raw rates summarize each workflow’s actual assigned cases:

| Workflow | Easy and hard weights | Calculation | Raw completion rate |
|---|---|---|---:|
| Old | 180/200 = 90% easy; 20/200 = 10% hard | (144 + 6) / 200, or 0.90 × 80% + 0.10 × 30% | **75%** |
| New | 20/200 = 10% easy; 180/200 = 90% hard | (18 + 72) / 200, or 0.10 × 90% + 0.90 × 40% | **45%** |

Thus the raw contrast is 45% − 75% = **−30 percentage points**. [A10-DATA]

Hard cases have lower completion rates under both workflows. They make up 90% of the new workflow’s cases but only 10% of the old workflow’s cases, consistent with the manager’s routing decision. Giving that lower-rate group much more weight pulls down the new workflow’s overall average enough to reverse the within-group comparison. [A10-DATA; A10-ASSIGNMENT]

The raw rates are valid descriptions of completion among the two groups that actually received each workflow. They do not compare the workflows under a common case mix.

### Standardizing to the two requested populations

A standardized rate averages each workflow’s complexity-specific rates using the same chosen complexity weights. It describes what those observed rates look like under a specified mix.

| Target case mix | Old workflow | New workflow | New minus old |
|---|---|---|---:|
| 50% easy, 50% hard | 0.50 × 80% + 0.50 × 30% = **55%** | 0.50 × 90% + 0.50 × 40% = **65%** | **+10 percentage points** |
| 20% easy, 80% hard | 0.20 × 80% + 0.80 × 30% = **40%** | 0.20 × 90% + 0.80 × 40% = **50%** | **+10 percentage points** |

The first pair describes a hypothetical population split equally between the recorded complexity groups. The second describes the requested expected next-quarter mix, using today’s observed rates as stipulated. These are derived summaries using [A10-DATA; A10-PLANNING].

Both differences are +10 percentage points because the observed difference is +10 in each stratum; any common set of easy/hard weights summing to one would preserve that difference here. The absolute rates still change with the mix.

The next-quarter calculation is not a demonstrated forecast of what an intervention will achieve. Holding the observed rates fixed is the committee’s calculation convention; changing routing, staff, workload, or the workflow itself could change those rates. [A10-PLANNING; A10-ASSIGNMENT]

### Assessing the three claims

**The director’s arithmetic is correct, but “harmful” is not established.** The 30-point raw decline compares different assigned populations. It does not show that the new workflow caused a case to be less likely to finish within seven days. The raw rates can still be useful for describing actual service performance under the observed routing arrangement.

**The implementation lead correctly identifies higher rates within both recorded complexity groups, but does not prove an improvement for every case—or even a causal average improvement.** Within each group, the manager also routed by urgency, which affects completion and is absent from the table. The specialist worked exclusively with the new workflow, and other staff differences may matter. Adjusting for complexity therefore leaves alternative explanations for the observed difference. Complexity occurring before assignment does not make it a complete account of why cases received different workflows. [A10-ASSIGNMENT]

Even a well-conducted randomized experiment showing a positive average effect would not show that every particular case benefits. Average gains can coexist with no benefit or harm for some cases, and a particular case’s outcomes under both alternatives are not jointly observed. Here, calling the adjusted +10-point difference the proven effect of switching a particular case adds both an unsupported causal claim and an unsupported individual guarantee.

**“Average the four percentages” needs a target population.** The unweighted average is:

(80% + 30% + 90% + 40%) / 4 = **60%**.

That calculation gives each workflow-by-complexity cell 25% weight. It can describe a hypothetical population equally distributed across those four cells, but it supplies no comparison between workflows. For the requested 50:50 comparison, average the two complexity rates separately for each workflow, producing 55% and 65%.

The 60% also happens to equal the actual pooled rate, 240 / 400. The pooled calculation’s actual cell weights are 45%, 5%, 5%, and 45%, in the table’s order—not 25% each. The equality of the answers does not make their weighting schemes interchangeable or establish a workflow effect. [A10-DATA]

### Evidence that would improve the rollout decision

The most direct next step is a prospective randomized comparison within easy and hard cases, designed around whether the committee intends to deploy the workflow alone or the workflow together with specialist staffing.

- **Random assignment within complexity groups** removes the manager’s ability to choose a workflow using urgency or other case characteristics. It balances such characteristics in expectation and supports estimating average effects of the assigned alternatives. If the new-workflow arm always receives the specialist while the old arm does not, the comparison estimates the effect of that combined workflow-and-staffing policy.
- **Measuring relevant pre-assignment variables**—especially urgency and factors influencing routing and staff availability—can support better comparisons in observational data and more informative randomized analyses. An observational causal interpretation still requires that the recorded variables cover the relevant common causes of assignment and completion, and that comparable cases actually receive both workflows. A larger table with the same missing routing information does not solve that problem.
- **Separating staff and workflow** asks a different question. If feasible, have comparable staff, including specialists, use both workflows, with case assignment randomized within complexity and staff groups. A design that independently varies workflow and specialist involvement can estimate their separate contributions and whether they work differently together. Exclusive pairing of the specialist with the new workflow cannot identify those separate contributions from this table.

If rollout will include the same specialist arrangement, its combined effect may be the decision-relevant quantity. If rollout will use ordinary staffing, evidence from a permanently specialist-only new arm would not directly establish that rollout’s effect. The study should represent the intended operating conditions and use the 20% easy/80% hard target mix when summarizing its estimated effects for next quarter.

The present numbers support clear descriptive comparisons. The unresolved decision question is what completion would change **because of the rollout**, after accounting for the routing and staffing differences identified in [A10-ASSIGNMENT].
