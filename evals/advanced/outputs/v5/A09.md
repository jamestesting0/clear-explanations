**A doubly positive part has about a 26.9% probability of being defective.** The memo’s roughly 75% result uses an incorrect joint false-positive rate. The second analyst is also wrong: B supplies additional information after A is positive, although much less than its marginal “sixteen-fold” figure suggests.

### Expected counts and probabilities

A 10,000-part shipment contains an expected **200 defective parts** and **9,800 nondefective parts**, using the supplied 2% defect rate. These are expected counts, not a guarantee about a particular shipment. [A09-BASE]

Multiply those population counts by the applicable conditional probabilities in the joint table:

| Observed result | Expected defective | Expected nondefective | Probability defective among parts with this result |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| A positive, regardless of B | 200 × 0.90 = 180 | 9,800 × 0.10 = 980 | 180 / 1,160 ≈ **15.5%** |
| A positive, B positive | 200 × 0.72 = 144 | 9,800 × 0.04 = 392 | 144 / 536 ≈ **26.9%** |
| A positive, B negative | 200 × 0.18 = 36 | 9,800 × 0.06 = 588 | 36 / 624 ≈ **5.77%** |

These calculations use [A09-BASE; A09-JOINT]. Each last-column denominator includes all parts with the specified detector result, because the question is their true defect status.

The marginal rates extracted by the memo are correct:

- A is positive for 0.72 + 0.18 = **90% of defective parts**, and 0.04 + 0.06 = **10% of nondefective parts**.
- B is positive for 0.72 + 0.08 = **80% of defective parts**, and 0.04 + 0.01 = **5% of nondefective parts**.

Those are the detectors’ sensitivity and false-positive rate, respectively. [A09-JOINT]

### Where the memo’s multiplication fails

Multiplying marginal rates requires the detectors’ outcomes to be independent within each true-status group. The defective column happens to satisfy the needed product: 0.90 × 0.80 = 0.72. The nondefective column does not:

- The memo assumes 0.10 × 0.05 = **0.005**, or a 0.5% double-positive rate among nondefective parts.
- The table gives **0.04**, or 4%—eight times as high. [A09-JOINT]

The memo therefore calculates only 9,800 × 0.005 = **49** nondefective double positives, instead of **392**. Its result, 144 / (144 + 49) ≈ **74.6%**, is correct arithmetic for that unsupported assumption. Using the supplied joint rate gives 144 / (144 + 392) ≈ **26.9%**.

### B must be evaluated inside the A-positive subset

Once A is known to be positive, the relevant question is how frequently B is positive **among A-positive parts of each true status**:

| Conditional rate inside the A-positive subset | Calculation | Result |
|---|---|---:|
| B positive among defective parts | 0.72 / 0.90, equivalently 144 / 180 | 80% |
| B positive among nondefective parts | 0.04 / 0.10, equivalently 392 / 980 | 40% |

Thus B’s positive **likelihood ratio** in this subset—the ratio of those two probabilities—is **0.80 / 0.40 = 2**. A positive B result is twice as likely for defective as for nondefective parts once A is positive. [A09-JOINT]

This multiplies the **odds**, meaning the ratio of defective to nondefective probabilities, by two:

- After A alone, the odds are 180:980 = 9:49.
- After both positives, the odds are 144:392 = 18:49.
- Converting those latter odds to a probability gives 18 / (18 + 49) = **26.9%**.

The marginal ratio 0.80 / 0.05 = 16 describes a B-positive result without already conditioning on A. It cannot be reused as B’s additional confirmation after A, because the nondefective A-positive subset has a 40% B-positive rate, not 5%.

Correlation therefore does not imply “literally no extra information.” B separates the A-positive group’s 15.5% probability into 26.9% for B-positive parts and 5.77% for B-negative parts. For completeness, within that subset B-negative rates are 20% for defective and 60% for nondefective parts, so a negative B result multiplies the odds by 0.20 / 0.60 = one-third. [A09-JOINT]

Running B only after A changes which results production observes. It does not invalidate these calculations, because the supplied validation study ran both detectors and had true-status labels, with representative conditional behavior for this incoming population. [A09-BASE; A09-JOINT]

### Turning probabilities into an inspection policy

A probability is evidence for a decision, not an automatic threshold. The missing inputs include the consequences of releasing a defective part, the cost and burden of inspection, inspection accuracy and what follows a defect finding, and available inspection capacity. Expected inspection volumes also matter: inspecting all A-positive parts would involve about 1,160 parts per shipment; requiring both positives would involve about 536, under these rates.

For illustration, if inspection were perfect, cost a fixed amount I per part, and prevent a missed-defect loss L, a simplified decision would compare I with probability-of-defect × L. Those costs and assumptions have not been supplied, and capacity constraints could change the policy.

If the incoming defect rate changes, recompute the probabilities using the new proportion of defective and nondefective parts even if detector behavior stays constant. If operating conditions, thresholds, or the kinds of parts within either status group change, revalidate the **joint conditional table**, including B’s behavior within A-positive parts. The current conclusions depend on its applicability to the incoming population and on reliable true-status labels. [A09-BASE; A09-JOINT]
