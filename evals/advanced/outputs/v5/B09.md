The passage invites us to read the narrator's accusation of Mara as a way of concentrating responsibility on someone else. Yet it withholds the evidence that would settle who physically opened the gate. Its uncertainty is carefully distributed: touching the mechanism, authorizing its use, and neglecting people downstream are different possible grounds of responsibility. The narrator repeatedly tries to make one suspicious detail answer all three questions. [B09-S1–S3]

### Certainty that exposes its own weakness

“I remember the gate opening because I remember being innocent” makes the narrator's claimed innocence the warrant for his memory. Ordinarily, a reliable account of what happened might establish innocence; here the desired moral conclusion appears to support the account. His confidence immediately encounters unstable chronology: the bell may not have sounded, or he may have heard a preliminary test. The qualification does not prove that his entire story is false, but it prevents confidence from functioning as corroboration. [B09-S1]

The missing boat gives this habit a particularly clear form. The narrator calls absence “proof” that his brother escaped, then admits that he once treated many absences that way. A boat's invisibility does not establish its occupant's safety. The later voice recognizes a weakness in the earlier voice, though that recognition is itself part of the narrator's present self-presentation. We receive both versions through him. [B09-S1]

The exchange about the key similarly redirects suspicion without resolving possession. His “You have the key” accuses Mara; her question about his repeated reaching toward an empty belt turns attention back to his bodily habit. That gesture could suggest a relationship to the missing key, but neither accusation establishes who actually had it when the gate opened. [B09-S1]

### Objects acquire accusations before they acquire proof

Mara's hidden hands make her appear unreadable. When a hand emerges red, the narrator wants the color to settle the question. The text immediately supplies competing explanations: the wheel is red, but so are the town's other public handles. A red palm cannot identify this particular contact, much less establish authorization, intention, or responsibility for the consequences. The handout's supposed universal code for guilt overlooks the passage's explicit weakening of that inference. [B09-S2, B09-S4]

“I wanted the color to be an accusation” reveals the narrator's investment more directly than the color reveals Mara's conduct. His preference for looking at her hand rather than toward the houses connects accusation with avoidance: focusing on one person's possible physical act is easier than facing the people exposed to harm. It suggests compromised responsibility without telling us whether a warning could still have saved them. [B09-S2]

The flat water, compared to an unopened letter, also makes what is visible seem insufficiently understood. As a possible image of unread communication, it gains force beside the unanswered question about warning the houses. That connection is interpretive; the simile supplies no factual report about a warning or the brother's fate. [B09-S1, B09-S2]

The keeper's daylight lantern offers another object that seems equipped to reveal something but adds no visibility. It can suggest the appearance of inquiry or authority without actual illumination. The draft instruction to keep it unnecessary supports the significance of that redundancy, but does not prescribe one definitive symbolic meaning. [B09-S2, B09-S4]

### Three questions of responsibility

The keeper asks who **requested** the opening, rather than who turned the wheel. “She did” and “We did” offer competing accounts of authorization. Mara's plural challenges the narrator's isolation of her, but remains a character's claim, not an independently confirmed account. Neither answer identifies the physical operator. [B09-S2]

The keeper records neither response and asks whether the lower houses were warned. That move introduces a third issue: even someone who did not touch the wheel might bear responsibility for approving the opening or failing to warn others. Conversely, identifying the operator would not by itself settle what the others knew or could have prevented. The passage leaves the warning question unanswered, and the editorial packet explicitly withholds confirmation that a timely warning was possible. [B09-S2, B09-S4]

### Time and records change the shape of accountability

The jump forward makes the narrator keeper himself, with authority to rewrite the public instruction. “No one opens this gate alone” can be heard as a practical prohibition on solitary action, but also as a broader demand for shared participation and responsibility. “Two witnesses required” specifies a number and an observational role. Witnesses might improve the evidence available afterward, yet their presence does not necessarily mean shared authorization, assistance, or care for the houses below. The clearer procedure can narrow the older demand by making witnessing appear sufficient. [B09-S3]

Keeping the old board under the bed, face down, creates a private counter-record to the public revision. It is preserved but hidden; its imagined speech in Mara's voice suggests that her claim of shared responsibility has not been silenced for the narrator. This can express guilt, unresolved attachment, or discomfort with the new rule. It does not identify the act for which he might be guilty. [B09-S3]

The ending distinguishes institutional time from remembered time: the records give noon; memory locates the opening at Mara's withdrawal from conversation. Those statements measure different things—clock time and emotional significance—and need not literally contradict each other. Neither answers the crucial causal question of when the shouting became audible. An apparently exact record can therefore leave moral knowledge incomplete. [B09-S3]

The discarded sentence “Mara turned the wheel” establishes what an earlier ending said, not a fact silently retained by the final passage. Its omission preserves the final text's uncertainty. Likewise, the jacket's “confession of a coward” offers a marketing interpretation, not independent testimony or an authoritative explanation of the narrator's guilt. [B09-S4]

We can thus read the passage as exposing self-protective accusation while preserving its unresolved questions: who operated the gate, whose authorization mattered, whether and when anyone could warn the houses, and whether the brother survived. Its strongest evidence concerns how the narrator handles responsibility; his handling of it cannot complete the missing history.
