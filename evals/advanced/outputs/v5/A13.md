### What the twenty-test calculation establishes

In the hypothetical experiment, all twenty null hypotheses are true and the twenty final p-values are independent and uniform on [0,1]. Each has a 0.95 probability of being at least 0.05. Therefore,

\[
P(\text{at least one false discovery})
=1-P(\text{all twenty }p\text{-values}\ge0.05)
=1-0.95^{20}
\approx\boxed{0.6415}.
\]

That is approximately **64.15%**, not 5%, for this explicitly specified hypothetical experiment. [A13-PLAN]

The *family-wise error rate* is the probability of at least one false rejection among the outcomes being tested. Bonferroni controls that rate at no more than 0.05 by testing each of twenty outcomes against

\[
\alpha/20=0.05/20=\boxed{0.0025}.
\]

Neither **0.018** nor **0.041** meets this threshold. The simple Bonferroni-adjusted value for the smallest final p-value is

\[
\min(1,20\times0.018)=\boxed{0.36}.
\]

Bonferroni does not require independence between the twenty outcomes, but it does require valid individual p-values for the testing procedure being used. The value 0.36 is the requested adjustment for the final twenty-outcome family; it does not also correct the unplanned stopping process. [A13-PLAN] [A13-RESULTS] [A13-LOOKS]

### Why the actual stopping process is a separate issue

The team inspected accumulating data four times before the final analysis and could stop or continue according to the results. Such a rule can change false-positive behavior because it creates repeated opportunities to select a favorable result. Its effect depends on the stopping rule and the dependence between results across looks. [A13-LOOKS]

The reviewer's expression \(1-0.95^{100}\) assumes one hundred independent opportunities with the stipulated null behavior. Five looks at the same accumulating participants do not supply that independence, and the packet explicitly limits the independence assumption to the twenty final p-values in the hypothetical calculation. Without the joint distribution and actual stopping decisions, **the exact overall error probability cannot be calculated**. ### A concrete confirmatory follow-up

Run a fresh randomized comparison assigning users to feature on or off. Before examining results:

1. **Fix the primary outcome:** average saved clicks per eligible session, with the saved-click definition, eligible sessions, follow-up window, and handling of repeated sessions specified. Analyze uncertainty in a way that accounts for sessions belonging to the same randomized user.
2. **Fix the stopping rule:** choose a sample size using a separately obtained variance estimate and a prespecified minimum worthwhile improvement; complete that sample and its follow-up without significance-driven extensions or early stopping. The packet lacks the variance needed to calculate that sample size now.
3. **Fix the statistical decision:** use one two-sided 5% primary test; claim evidence for an increase only if the estimate is positive and its corresponding 95% confidence interval lies entirely above zero. Any other outcomes used for confirmatory claims need their own prespecified multiplicity control.
4. **Fix the product criteria:** report the effect and confidence interval against the minimum worthwhile improvement, and measure task completion time as a prespecified secondary outcome to assess actual effort saved. A statistically detectable increase in saved clicks alone should not settle the launch decision.

This follow-up would provide independent evidence for a defined claim, with uncertainty and practical value assessed under decisions made before the results are known.
