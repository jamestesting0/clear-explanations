You multiply by each group’s fraction because you want the **average uncertainty per example**, and a bigger group contains more of the examples you are averaging over.

Here, entropy measures uncertainty about an example’s label. A group where every label is the same has entropy zero; a group evenly split between two labels has entropy one bit, using base-2 logarithms.

Suppose a split divides 100 examples like this:

| Group | Examples | Entropy within the group | Contribution |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| A: all the same label | 90 | 0 bits | 0.9 × 0 = 0 |
| B: evenly split between two labels | 10 | 1 bit | 0.1 × 1 = 0.1 |

The average uncertainty after learning which group an example belongs to is **0.1 bits per example**.

Why those weights? Choose one of the original 100 examples at random. There is a 90% chance it comes from A and only a 10% chance it comes from B. Your average must reflect those chances.

Simply averaging the two entropies would give `(0 + 1) / 2 = 0.5`. That treats the two groups as equally common, even though A has nine times as many examples.

For a decision-tree split, the denominator is the number of examples at the node being split, not necessarily the entire training dataset.
