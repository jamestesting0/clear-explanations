**No. OAuth and JWT do different jobs and can be used together.** OAuth defines how an application obtains permission to access a resource; JWT defines a format for carrying information in a token.

**OAuth 2.0** is an authorization framework: a set of rules for granting access. For example, you could authorize a photo-printing app to read your photos from another service without giving the printing app your password. The app receives an **access token**, which it presents when requesting your photos.

A **JWT**—JSON Web Token—is a way to represent information such as an account identifier, permissions, or an expiration time. JWTs are commonly signed, allowing a receiver to check that the contents have not been altered. A signature does not hide the contents; a signed JWT is generally readable unless it is also encrypted.

An OAuth access token might be a JWT. It could instead be an opaque token: a value whose meaning the service looks up rather than reads from the token itself. OAuth does not require JWTs.

Using a JWT alone does not provide OAuth’s process for granting access. And OAuth itself is about authorization; OpenID Connect adds a standardized way to establish the user’s identity for sign-in.
