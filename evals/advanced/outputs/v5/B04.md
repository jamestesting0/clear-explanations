The trial shows **less expired stock and higher contribution across the two stores**, but it does not establish better demand forecasts or preserved customer service. The same totals could arise from improved matching, underordering at North, changed customer demand, or directing more stock toward South's new customer.

### What the arithmetic establishes

Because opening and closing stock were zero and transfers were prohibited, each store's receipts equal sales plus discards. The ledger therefore accounts for all supplied items. [B04-S1]

| Store | Items received: baseline → trial | Items sold: baseline → trial | Items discarded: baseline → trial | Hours without stock: baseline → trial |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| North | 1,000 → 800 | 900 → 760 | 100 → 40 | 2 → 18 |
| South | 400 → 600 | 360 → 570 | 40 → 30 | 1 → 1 |
| Total | 1,400 → 1,400 | 1,260 → 1,330 | 140 → 70 | 3 → 19 |

Using those counts, expired waste fell by 70 items, or **50% relative to the original 140**. Since total receipts stayed at 1,400, the discarded share also halved, from 10% to 5%. That is a five-percentage-point decrease. It is not a measurement of forecast accuracy: the packet supplies neither forecasts nor a complete measure of the demand those forecasts were meant to predict. [B04-S1]

Sales divided by receipts rose from 90% to 95%, both at each store and overall. This measures the share of supplied stock that sold. It does not measure the share of customers whose requests were fulfilled. Ordering fewer items can raise that ratio while leaving more customers unable to buy. [B04-S1; B04-S2]

North was without stock for 1% of opening hours before the trial and 9% during it. South remained at 0.5%. Across 400 store-hours per period, time without stock rose from 0.75% to 4.75%. These are availability measures, not percentages of disappointed customers: one busy hour can involve many more purchase attempts than another. Nevertheless, North's worsening availability directly challenges an unqualified claim that service was preserved. [B04-S2]

### Contribution increased by 700 currency units

Contribution here is sales revenue minus the acquisition cost of **all items received**, including those that expired:

\[
\text{Contribution}=10\times\text{items sold}-6\times\text{items received}.
\]

| Store | Baseline contribution | Trial contribution | Change |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| North | 3,000 | 2,800 | −200 |
| South | 1,200 | 2,100 | +900 |
| Total | **4,200** | **4,900** | **+700** |

For example, trial contribution is \(10(1,330)-6(1,400)=4,900\). Aggregate revenue rose from 12,600 to 13,300, while aggregate acquisition cost remained 8,400. Contribution therefore rose by 700, approximately 16.7% of the baseline contribution. Disposal cost and other unchanged expenses are excluded as requested; their unknown amounts prevent calculating the absolute result after those expenses. These calculations follow from the ledger and prices, not from an attribution of the gain to the forecasting system. [B04-S1]

### Several causal stories fit the evidence

**Better matching is possible.** The system may have reduced excess supply at North and increased supply where South could sell it. But the intervention coincided with changes that could produce the same observed pattern. North lost a nearby bus stop, potentially changing customer numbers and arrival times; South acquired a cafeteria customer that bought 120 items. Weather and events also changed. Both stores adopted together, leaving no contemporary comparison showing what would have happened without the system. [B04-S3]

**North may have ordered too little, or received stock at the wrong times.** Its receipts fell by 200 and sales fell by 140 while stockout hours increased. More time without stock does not prove total demand rose: lower supply or a different timing of demand can produce more shortages even if total demand is unchanged or lower. The two notes about long queues suggest pressure at particular moments, but cannot establish demand over four weeks. [B04-S1; B04-S2]

**South's new business may explain some of the aggregate improvement.** Its sales rose by 210, with 120 trial sales identified as cafeteria purchases. The remaining 450 sales exceed baseline sales by 90, but that residual is not an estimate of the system's effect. Nor can we assume the cafeteria's purchases would simply subtract from an otherwise unchanged store: its presence may have affected ordering and availability. [B04-S1; B04-S3]

Finally, selecting stores because waste was unusually high makes a subsequent decline less decisive. An unusually bad period may be followed by a more ordinary one without an effective intervention. The marketing statement about predictability supplies no evidence about these stores. [B04-S2; B04-S3]

### What is missing about demand?

Demand here means the purchases customers would have wanted to make if the item had been available under the relevant trading conditions. Completed sales record only the fulfilled part.

Stockout durations do not reveal how many purchase attempts occurred during them, how many shoppers substituted, or how many returned later. The records therefore cannot supply a numerical estimate of unmet demand, establish a positive minimum for lost purchases, or provide a useful upper bound. Multiplying stockout hours by the average sales rate would require an unsupported assumption that demand during unavailable hours resembled demand during available hours. The queue notes do not repair that gap. [B04-S2]

Because training used sales rather than attempted purchases, the system may learn that stock-limited periods had low demand. That is a possible mechanism for repeated underordering, not proof that it happened here. [B04-S3]

### A feasible next evaluation

Expand as a controlled trial: assign comparable stores to the system or existing practice by chance, run both groups over the same periods, and include stores with different customer patterns. Record new contracts, transport disruptions, and other changes. Avoid choosing only stores coming off exceptionally high waste.

Alongside receipts, expiry, revenue, and costs, collect purchase attempts, unavailable-item requests, substitutions, and the timing of stockouts. Define success beforehand as lower waste or higher contribution **with an acceptable level of fulfilled demand**, and assess results separately for customer patterns such as regular contract orders and walk-in trade.

Lower waste with stable fulfillment would support improved matching. Lower waste accompanied by more unfilled requests would reveal underordering. Gains concentrated in newly contracted demand would limit the case for expansion to other store types.

Even a successful reconstruction of all past demand would answer only how much demand existed. It would not establish what ordering, waste, service, and contribution would have been without the system. The contemporary randomized comparison is what addresses that causal question.
