## What would make that contrast causal?

The missing quantity is North's after-period outcome **without the tool**. That unobserved alternative is the counterfactual. To interpret all 12 points as the tool's effect, the design would need a credible reason to believe that, without the tool, North's outcome would have changed by the same four points as South over these periods, with no other North-specific change contributing to the contrast.

This is the relevant **parallel-trends assumption**: the regions would have had equal changes in the absence of the intervention. They need not have equal outcome levels. A persistent regional gap cancels when changes are compared; a gap that would have grown anyway does not.

The observed pre-period pattern challenges that assumption:

| Pre-period transition | North's increase | South's increase | Increase in North–South gap |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Week −3 to −2 | 4 | 1 | 3 |
| Week −2 to −1 | 4 | 1 | 3 |

North was already improving three points per week faster. Thus North's 16-point change cannot simply be assigned to the tool, and subtracting South's four-point change does not automatically remove North's existing differential improvement. [A23-SERIES; A23-CONTEXT]

These two pre-period transitions do not prove what North's future untreated trajectory would have been. Trends can change. They do, however, give a concrete reason to question an unadjusted parallel-trends interpretation rather than accepting it by default.

## Why the detrended calculation is six points

The average time in the before period is week `−1.5`; in the after period it is week `0.5`. They are **two weeks apart**, so extending the three-point weekly trend difference predicts six points of gap growth even without a new differential effect:

\[
\text{Adjusted contrast}=12-(3\text{ points/week}\times2\text{ weeks})
=\boxed{6\text{ points}}.
\]

A literal extrapolation of each region's pretrend gives the same result:

| Region | Extrapolated week 0 | Extrapolated week 1 | Extrapolated after mean | Supplied after mean minus extrapolated mean |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| North: add 4 each week | 92 | 96 | 94 | `102 − 94 = 8` |
| South: add 1 each week | 53 | 54 | 53.5 | `55.5 − 53.5 = 2` |

Subtracting these departures gives `8 − 2 = 6`. [A23-SERIES]

The adjustment adds a modeling assumption: the untreated difference in trends would have continued at exactly three points per week. It does not turn that hypothetical trajectory into an observation. The remaining six points could still reflect the tool, staffing, changing case mix, other North-specific changes, or an incorrect extrapolation. The numerical adjustment also does not repair the impossible percentage in the source data.

## Staffing and the pretrend test

**The simultaneous staffing change prevents separation of the interventions.** North received both the tool and five experienced temporary staff at week zero, while South received neither. Even if a suitable trend model isolated a causal North-specific change, this comparison would not tell us how much came from the tool, staffing, or the way they work together. Different customer groups and uninvestigated changes in case mix add further uncertainty. [A23-CONTEXT]

**A nonsignificant pretrend test does not prove parallel trends.** Such a test may fail to detect a meaningful difference when its evidence is weak or its model is unsuitable. Here, its model, uncertainty, and sample size were not retained, so its power—the ability to detect relevant departures—cannot be assessed. Nor can the supplied five rows establish what would have happened after rollout without the intervention. No standard error, significance level, or confidence interval can responsibly be recovered from this packet alone. [A23-CLAIMS; A23-ANALYSIS]

## A follow-up that informs the expansion decision

Use the stated ability to randomize future rollout timing across additional comparable centers. For a **tool-only comparison**, hold staffing policy constant and randomly assign centers to receive the tool now or later. Compare outcomes over the same calendar periods, before the delayed group receives the tool. This would provide a concurrent comparison for the tool without repeating North's staffing confound. The completed North rollout need not be undone.

To distinguish all expansion options directly, **if staffing allocation can also be randomized**, independently assign tool timing and additional staffing to create four groups:

| Group | Tool | Additional experienced staff |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Comparison | No, during the comparison period | No |
| Tool only | Yes | No |
| Staffing only | No, during the comparison period | Yes |
| Combined | Yes | Yes |

Tool-only versus comparison estimates the tool's effect without extra staff. Combined versus comparison estimates the package's effect. Combined versus tool-only estimates what adding staff contributes when the tool is present; combined versus staffing-only estimates what the tool contributes when extra staff are present. These contrasts also allow the two interventions to work differently together rather than assuming their effects simply add.

If staffing cannot be randomized, tool timing can still be randomized separately among centers with and without additional staff. That identifies the tool's incremental effect within those staffing conditions, but the staffing and package comparisons remain dependent on further assumptions; recording staffing does not itself remove selection bias.

Collect multiple pre- and post-rollout periods, actual staffing and tool-use records, case mix, ticket counts, and ticket-level outcomes with a complete two-day observation window. Prespecify the outcome, comparison periods, and whether centers, weeks, or tickets receive equal weight. Account for assignment at the center level when estimating uncertainty, since tickets sharing a center also share staffing and processes.
