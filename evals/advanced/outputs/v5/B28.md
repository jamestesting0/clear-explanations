**The experiment supports a causal comparison within teams where exactly half the employees were assigned training. It does not directly compare training everyone with training nobody.** Sharing changes what the randomized comparison means; it does not make randomization meaningless.

## The observed comparison

Assigned employees made \(160/2{,}000=8\%\) errors; unassigned employees made \(200/2{,}000=10\%\). The observed assigned-minus-unassigned difference is **−2 percentage points**, or a 20% reduction relative to the unassigned rate. This is an estimate from one randomized allocation, not an established exact effect for future employees. [B28-S1]

Employees' training invitations were randomized within each team. Their actual exposure to the checklist was not: at least six unassigned employees received explanations, and neither their complete contacts nor their learning is known. The comparison therefore concerns **assignment to training**, not a clean separation between people who learned the checklist and people who did not. Reclassifying people by whether they received explanations would lose the original randomized comparison. [B28-S1, B28-S2]

The 4,000 checks are not 4,000 independently randomized employees. With only aggregate outcomes and no uncertainty estimates, the packet does not establish the comparison's precision or statistical significance. [B28-S1]

## Why peers change the causal question

A *spillover* occurs when one employee's training assignment affects another employee's performance. Sharing could help colleagues; time spent in training could reduce the help available to them. The size and direction of these effects are unknown. [B28-S2]

Exactly five assignments per team creates this unavoidable difference:

| Employee's assignment | Other assigned teammates |
| --- | ---: |
| Assigned | 4 |
| Unassigned | 5 |

Randomization supports a contrast between being assigned with four assigned peers and being unassigned with five, averaging over the randomized identities of those peers. It does **not** isolate changing one's own assignment while keeping every peer's assignment fixed: under the five-person quota, changing one person's status requires changing somebody else's. Identities may matter as well as counts. [B28-S1, B28-S2]

Consequently, the observed gap mixes the consequences of own assignment with differences in peer assignments. Sharing might narrow that gap if unassigned employees benefit, but time competition or other responses could change it differently. There is no established direction of bias relative to a universal-versus-none rollout effect—and that effect is a different comparison in the first place. [B28-S2, B28-S4]

## Why the other comparisons do not fill the gap

The historical comparison, \(20\%-8\%=12\) percentage points, crosses periods and task versions. It cannot separate training from changes in assessment or other changes over time. The distant teams' decline from 18% to 16% supplies no randomized substitute: their tasks and baseline experience differ, and the packet does not establish that their trend represents what would have happened in the experimental teams. Neither comparison supplies randomized observations of universal versus zero training. [B28-S3]

## What a further study could answer

Randomly assigning many teams to zero, partial, or full training would address several distinct questions. [B28-S4]

- **Overall rollout effect:** Compare average outcomes in full-training and zero-training teams. This includes the combined effects of own training and coworkers' training under the study's working conditions.
- **Spillovers:** Compare unassigned employees in partial-training teams with employees in zero-training teams. Their own assignment is unchanged, while exposure to assigned coworkers differs.
- **Own-training effects:** Randomizing individuals within partial-training teams preserves a useful assignment comparison. However, fixed partial quotas still tie own assignment to peers' assignments. Isolating own assignment with the same distribution of peer assignments would require a design that permits that comparison, such as independently randomizing individual invitations rather than fixing the number per team.

These interpretations also require handling possible sharing across teams; otherwise even zero-training teams might receive information from trained teams. The study should retain the workplace conditions relevant to the intended rollout.

Forbidding communication could reduce checklist sharing, but it would test training under imposed isolation. It would also remove ordinary opportunities to help coworkers, and would not automatically remove every possible peer effect. That design answers a different question from introducing training into a workplace where collaboration continues. [B28-S2, B28-S4]
