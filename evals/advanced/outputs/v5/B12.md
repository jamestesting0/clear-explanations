The copying history establishes that East and West both continue Vale's memories and unfinished project. It does not establish that either is uniquely the same person as Vale. Nevertheless, the community's explicit rule settles the project's practical obligation: **East and West jointly owe one completed map or one repayment of the advance**. That allocation does not settle personal identity, ownership of other property, or blame for later conduct. [B12-S1, B12-S3]

### Four questions that need different answers

“The same person” can mean **numerical identity**: literally one and the same individual, rather than two individuals who resemble each other. Memory concerns psychological continuity—retaining a predecessor's experiences, commitments, and perspective. Ownership concerns rights to particular things. Responsibility can mean an outstanding duty, or it can mean blame for an action. None of these relations automatically supplies all the others.

East and West sincerely remember making the promise. Because copying gave both that memory, its sincerity cannot uniquely identify either as Vale. Their common memories also do not eliminate their present distinction: they occupy separate machines, act independently, and acquire experiences the other does not share. [B12-S1]

That distinction creates a precise problem for ordinary identity. Let \(V\) denote Vale, \(E\) East, and \(W\) West. If \(E=V\) and \(W=V\), then symmetry and transitivity imply \(E=W\). Transitivity means that if the first is identical to the second, and the second to the third, the first and third are identical. But the case stipulates two distinct successors. We therefore cannot maintain all three claims while using ordinary numerical identity. [B12-S1]

A theory might identify only one successor with Vale, identify neither, or argue that a branching continuity relation matters more than strict identity for some purposes. What it cannot do is call both distinct successors numerically identical to Vale without explaining a departure from the ordinary identity relation. The technical report supplies no privileged relation that selects East over West. [B12-S1]

### Facts, criteria, and the speakers' inferences

East's premise—remembering the advance—is supported. His conclusion that he **alone** owes the work is not. West correctly points out that the same memory gives East no advantage over him. But equal psychological evidence does not itself prove that both are strictly Vale, or dictate how obligations must be allocated. [B12-S1, B12-S2]

The neighbor's objection expresses a concern about multiplying an obligation just because there are more successors. The community rule adopts that concern by allowing only one satisfaction of the debt. This is an allocation principle; counting one original promise does not by itself prove a metaphysical theory about its successors. [B12-S2, B12-S3]

The treasurer assumes that obligations can continue only through strict personal identity. That is a proposed criterion for responsibility, not a consequence of the copying facts. It also misreads this community's actual rule, which attaches the obligation to authorized successors whether or not they are strictly the original. Even granting the treasurer's hypothetical premise that neither is Vale would not trigger forgiveness under the supplied rule. [B12-S2, B12-S3]

The spectator makes a different error: a prediction about behavior. Shared earlier memories do not guarantee identical future choices when later information and experiences differ. East has already received information West has not. This undermines the spectator's inference without requiring any decision about personhood. [B12-S1, B12-S2]

The proposed theories emphasize different criteria or values:

- **Theory A** requires continuous physical implementation. Copying memories is insufficient to meet that requirement by itself; the relevant question is whether any successor satisfies the demanded physical continuity.
- **Theory B** prioritizes enough psychological continuity to carry projects forward, allowing that continuity to branch. It can explain why both successors matter for Vale's projects without establishing that both are numerically Vale.
- **Theory C** gives public recognition a role in constituting identity. The community's practices would therefore matter to that theory, but this particular rule explicitly declines to decide identity. Allocating work to both is not an implicit declaration that both are one person.

These are offered positions, not conclusions established by the report. The chamber's “you live twice” brochure supplies no argument for choosing among them, and the earlier cartography award supplies no evidence about copying or the rule's scope. [B12-S3, B12-S4]

### What the rule actually resolves

Both successors qualify because Vale's documented continuity plan deliberately named both. They therefore share an obligation to provide **one complete map or one full repayment**. They may divide the work or repayment internally; the rule does not mandate equal halves. Once the community receives either complete satisfaction, it cannot demand another simply because there are two successors. [B12-S3]

That answers who owes this performance and what discharges it. It does not determine ownership of unrelated property, specify punishment, or establish that one successor possesses all Vale's former rights. A duty to repay is not itself a complete theory of who owns which assets. [B12-S3]

### Why later blame needs its own argument

Suppose East later harms someone. Assessing West's blame would require facts about West's own knowledge, choices, assistance, authorization, control, or relevant duties—not merely the fact that both descend from Vale. Separate later experiences mean West need not even know what East has learned or done. Shared pre-copy memory provides no automatic link of participation in a later act. [B12-S1]

West might become responsible through a new agreement, knowing assistance, or a culpable failure to perform a relevant duty, but those grounds would need to be established. The same reasoning applies in the other direction. The project's joint obligation gives both a duty toward its completion; it does not turn every later action of either successor into the other's action, nor supply a punishment policy that the community never adopted.
