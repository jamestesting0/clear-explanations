Both reported patterns are arithmetically correct: **seven-day success rose within each difficulty group, while the overall rate fell**. The tool period contained a much larger share of Hard requests, whose success rate remained lower than Easy requests. These observations do not isolate the tool's causal effect, and the packet supplies no district-specific results from which to assess fairness. [B10-S1, B10-S2, B10-S4]

### The overall rate depends on the mix

Here, “success” means completion within seven days **among accepted requests**. A satisfactory repair completed later still counts as a failure for this particular measure. [B10-S1]

| Accepted requests | Baseline successes / accepted | Baseline rate | Tool-period successes / accepted | Tool-period rate |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Easy | 90 / 100 | 90% | 19 / 20 | 95% |
| Hard | 20 / 100 | 20% | 60 / 200 | 30% |
| Overall | 110 / 200 | 55% | 79 / 220 | 35.9% |

Calculated from [B10-S1].

The baseline gave Easy and Hard requests equal weight. In the tool period, only \(20/220\approx9.1\%\) of accepted requests were Easy; roughly 90.9% were Hard. Thus the tool-period overall rate lies much closer to the Hard rate of 30%:

\[
\frac{20}{220}(95\%)+\frac{200}{220}(30\%)
=\frac{79}{220}\approx35.9\%.
\]

The overall rate fell by about **19.1 percentage points**, despite Easy improving by 5 points and Hard by 10 points. An overall average is pulled toward the group containing more requests. Improving both group rates cannot guarantee an improving overall rate when those weights also change. [B10-S1]

The first presentation therefore mistakes changing weights for an arithmetic error. The second makes an equally unsupported leap from an overall decline to harm to every type of customer. The observed category rates both improved, and neither comparison establishes what the tool caused. [B10-S1–S3]

### Reweighting answers a useful descriptive question

Using the baseline's fifty-fifty mix, the reweighted tool-period rate is:

\[
0.5(95\%)+0.5(30\%)=62.5\%.
\]

This is **7.5 percentage points above** the baseline's 55%. It answers: “What weighted average do the observed tool-period category rates produce if we give Easy and Hard their baseline weights?” It removes the effect of changing the relative sizes of these two recorded categories from that comparison. It is not the tool period's observed overall rate. [B10-S1, B10-S3]

Nor is it automatically a prediction of what would happen if the network actually changed its accepted mix. Such a change might affect capacity, selection, or the category rates themselves. The calculation simply holds the two observed rates fixed while changing their weights.

### Why this does not identify the tool's benefit

To identify a causal effect, the comparison must separate the tool from other reasons outcomes could differ. The contract and technician training changed at the same time as the tool. Acceptance decisions also changed, managers' override reasons are unavailable, and Hard requests may differ in unrecorded severity across periods. A fixed category definition ensures that the label means the same thing; it does not ensure that the requests inside that category are otherwise comparable. [B10-S2]

Reweighting Easy versus Hard cannot correct those differences within categories or disentangle concurrent changes. With no randomized assignment or concurrent untreated comparison, calling 62.5% the tool's “causal benefit” overstates the evidence. [B10-S2, B10-S3]

The recorded number of seven-day successes also fell, from 110 to 79, while accepted requests rose from 200 to 220. Those are counts for the supplied periods, not estimates of all satisfactory repairs or unmet demand. We lack rejected and abandoned requests' counts and outcomes, and the packet does not establish equal period lengths. We therefore cannot infer a comparable success rate per unit of time, how many people needed service, or how many eventually obtained satisfactory repairs. [B10-S1, B10-S2]

### Evaluate access and completion together

Equal success rates among accepted requests would not establish fair access. Two districts could have equal outcomes for those admitted while facing different acceptance decisions for comparable incoming requests. The packet contains no district cross-tabulation, so this is a reason to measure access, not evidence that such a disparity occurred. The policy also needs an agreed definition of “comparable.” [B10-S4]

A defensible prospective evaluation would have four parts:

1. **Record the full incoming population.** Retain district, arrival time, repair characteristics measured before the decision, tool recommendation, actual acceptance or rejection, abandonment, and override reasons. Follow outcomes for accepted and unaccepted requests where possible. This makes access measurable and prevents accepted customers from standing in for everyone seeking service.
2. **Define a district comparison before inspecting results.** Agree which repair characteristics legitimately affect eligibility, such as type and assessed severity. For East and West, compare acceptance proportions within those same categories and severity levels, then summarize them using a shared incoming-request mix. State in advance how large an unexplained acceptance gap warrants review, and report counts and uncertainty. District itself should not silently substitute for an eligibility reason.
3. **Report separate customer outcomes.** Alongside acceptance gaps, compare waiting time before acceptance, seven-day completion among accepted requests, eventual satisfactory repair, and successful repairs among all incoming requests. Use comparable observation periods and follow-up. Report district results on the same measures; equal conditional completion rates cannot erase an access gap.
4. **Create a credible tool comparison.** Where feasible, prospectively randomize comparable requests or sites between tool-assisted and existing manual decisions, under the same contract, training, and eligibility policy. Record overrides and analyze outcomes by original assignment across all incoming requests. Restricting the analysis to accepted requests could reintroduce selection because the tool helps determine acceptance itself. If randomization is unavailable, a concurrent comparison with detailed severity and selection records can improve the evidence, while leaving stronger causal assumptions explicit.

This proposal makes the policy's access requirement testable and evaluates the tool against the customer council's wider goals, without treating the current seven-day proportion as a complete measure of either customer benefit or fairness. [B10-S2, B10-S4]
