The low reported latency and the large backlog can both be real: **the latency histogram describes jobs that finished, while the unfinished-job gauge also includes jobs that remain stuck**. The supplied evidence does not yet justify doubling the workers; a blocking dependency could leave many jobs unfinished even when CPU use is low. [B08-S1–S3]

### The flow counts agree

Every admitted job must either complete or remain unfinished, since none were canceled, dropped, or transferred. The balance is:

\[
120\text{ initially unfinished}+600\text{ admissions}-540\text{ completions}
=180\text{ finally unfinished}.
\]

Over the 600-second window, the measured **arrival rate** was \(600/600=1\) job per second. The **completion rate**, or throughput, was \(540/600=0.9\) jobs per second. Their difference accounts for the backlog increasing by 60 jobs. Calling 0.9 the arrival rate confuses two different counters. [B08-S1, B08-S4]

The gauge's mean of 150 is a time-weighted average: it describes how many unfinished jobs were present across the window. It was measured continuously; the matching value of \((120+180)/2\) does not establish that the backlog grew uniformly. [B08-S1]

### The latency statistics use a different population

The 20-second mean averages the full admission-to-completion durations of **540 completed jobs**. Those durations include any time spent in the system before the window. The 25-second ninety-fifth percentile concerns that same completion sample. Neither statistic includes the 180 jobs still unfinished at the end. [B08-S2]

Consequently, the percentile does not establish that 95 percent of the 600 newly admitted jobs finished within 25 seconds. Some of the 540 completions may belong to the 120 jobs present initially, and unfinished jobs have no completed duration in this histogram. A long-blocked job can keep adding to the unfinished count while contributing nothing to the completion-latency statistics. [B08-S1, B08-S2]

This selection matters especially here: one class continued normally while a shared dependency intermittently blocked another. Fast completed jobs may therefore be a poor guide to the jobs left behind. The packet does not identify all remaining jobs' classes, so it does not establish that the dependency explains the entire backlog. [B08-S3]

### Why multiplying the two averages fails

The relationship the manager has heard is commonly written \(L=\lambda W\): average number in the system equals arrival rate times average time spent there. To apply it as a long-run relationship, the counts and durations must describe the same system and population, with sufficiently stable flow and an observation horizon that handles jobs crossing its boundaries. A short window can instead require explicit boundary accounting.

Here, \(L\) counts all unfinished jobs throughout ten minutes, while \(W=20\) seconds describes only that window's departures and includes some time outside the window. Arrivals also exceed completions. Multiplying \(1\times20\) therefore does not produce a valid prediction of this gauge. The discrepancy alone falsifies neither instrument. [B08-S1, B08-S2]

There is nevertheless an exact accounting check that constrains what could have happened. The gauge implies:

\[
150\times600=90{,}000\text{ job-seconds of unfinished work present in the window}.
\]

The 540 completers have only \(540\times20=10{,}800\) job-seconds of total residence time, some potentially before the window. Their contribution **inside** the window can therefore be no greater than 10,800 job-seconds. The 180 jobs still unfinished must account for at least:

\[
90{,}000-10{,}800=79{,}200\text{ job-seconds}.
\]

That is at least 88 percent of the window's accumulated unfinished-job time, or an average of at least 440 seconds spent inside this window per ending job. This is feasible within a 600-second window, but it rules out explanations in which almost all ending jobs arrived only moments before the cutoff. The summaries permit a substantial persistent backlog; they do not make every possible job history equally plausible. These bounds are calculated from [B08-S1, B08-S2].

### What would support a scaling decision?

The estimate \(180/0.9=200\) seconds is a **conditional drain calculation**: admissions must stop and completions must continue at 0.9 jobs per second until the backlog is empty. The measured throughput does not guarantee that condition. Once the normally completing class stops arriving, the remaining work may be dominated by dependency-blocked jobs. Even a stable average throughput would not promise an exact clearing time. [B08-S3, B08-S4]

The most useful additional measurements are:

1. **Ages, stages, and classes of unfinished jobs**, alongside per-class admissions and completions. Follow admission cohorts through completion, retaining the ages of jobs still unfinished, so fast departures cannot hide slow survivors.
2. **Actual worker demand and occupancy**: jobs waiting for an available worker, slots actively executing, slots held while waiting, and CPU use during execution. The unfinished count is not slot occupancy because pending external requests can release their slots. Low CPU alone does not resolve whether execution capacity is sufficient. [B08-S3]
3. **Dependency behavior by job class**: request duration, outstanding requests, timeouts, retries, and rate-limit responses. These would connect the suspected blocker to the jobs that are accumulating; the dependency team's missing data is material. [B08-S3]

More workers would address a queue waiting for scarce execution capacity. They would not directly make an unresponsive dependency answer, and could increase pressure on a limited dependency. Use the measurements to locate the waiting, then test a small capacity change if worker shortage remains plausible. Quarterly uptime says nothing decisive about this window's job flow or completion delays. [B08-S3, B08-S4]
