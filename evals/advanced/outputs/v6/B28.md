**The randomized comparison is informative about assignment under the five-per-team scheme, but it does not establish the effect of training everyone rather than nobody.** Sharing and competition for colleagues' time mean that an employee's outcome can depend on other people's assignments as well as their own. [B28-S1, B28-S2]

## The observed difference and its target

Assigned employees made \(160/2,000=8\%\) errors; unassigned employees made \(200/2,000=10\%\). The observed difference is **2 percentage points lower** for assigned employees, equivalent to a 20% relative reduction from the unassigned group's rate. These are comparisons of the recorded outcomes, not proof that a future rollout will reproduce those numbers. [B28-S1]

Randomization concerned employees' **training invitations**. It did not randomly determine all the training or information they actually received. At least six unassigned employees received checklist explanations, and the packet does not report complete training uptake or subsequent exposure. The comparison therefore concerns assignment, often called an *intention-to-treat* comparison; it does not directly estimate the effect of actually learning or using the checklist. [B28-S1, B28-S2]

The fixed allocation creates a further distinction:

| An employee's assignment | Other assigned teammates |
| --- | ---: |
| Assigned | 4 |
| Unassigned | 5 |

Thus the randomized comparison averages over two different joint conditions: being assigned with four assigned teammates, versus being unassigned with five. It also averages over which teammates receive invitations. It can estimate a causal contrast between those conditions under this allocation design. It does **not** isolate changing one's own invitation while leaving every peer's assignment unchanged. [B28-S1, B28-S2]

The observed two-point difference is one realization of that randomized comparison. The report provides no uncertainty estimate or employee-level outcomes from which to assess its precision in the usual way. The 4,000 checks are not 4,000 independent random assignments: assignment occurred for 40 employees, with fixed totals within four teams. [B28-S1]

## Why sharing neither ruins randomization nor has a known bias direction

An effect on someone caused by a colleague's assignment is a **spillover**. Explanations shared with unassigned employees could improve their performance. Training time could also reduce the help colleagues receive, and other responses could change either group's outcomes. The packet establishes some sharing, but not the net size or direction of these effects. [B28-S2]

It is therefore unjustified to say that sharing must make the estimate too small. That claim would require additional assumptions about how exposure affects each group and about which causal effect is being compared. It is equally unjustified to call the experiment useless: randomization still supports the allocation-specific comparison. The limitation is that this comparison answers a different question from universal training versus no training. Neither of those whole-team conditions was randomized or observed within the experimental design. [B28-S1, B28-S2, B28-S4]

## Why the other comparisons do not supply the missing answer

Subtracting the current assigned rate of 8% from the historical 20% gives a 12-point change across different task versions and times. Without a comparable contemporaneous alternative, that change cannot be attributed to training. Differences in task difficulty or other changes could contribute. [B28-S3]

The distant teams' decline from 18% to 16% also does not repair the problem. Those teams were not randomized into the comparison, had different experience, and used different tasks. Treating their trend as what would have happened in the experimental teams requires comparability assumptions that the packet does not establish. Neither historical rates nor this distant trend reveals outcomes under universal and zero training for the experimental setting. [B28-S3]

## What a next study could answer

Randomizing many teams to zero, partial, or full training would create the missing policy comparison. Comparing full-training and zero-training teams would estimate the effect of those assignment policies, with uncertainty assessed at the team level. This addresses rollout much more directly if the assessments are comparable and teams contain the relevant interactions. If substantial sharing occurs across teams, the design must account for that or randomize larger groups; otherwise it still falls short of a company-wide contrast. [B28-S4; design implications]

Unassigned employees in partial-training teams could be compared with employees in zero-training teams to estimate spillovers from colleagues' invitations. However, within a partial-training team with a fixed assigned count, the four-versus-five issue remains: individual randomization alone does not separate one's own effect while holding peers fixed. To target that distinction, the design could allow an employee's invitation to vary without forcing another teammate's invitation to change—for example, through independent individual invitations—and compare own assignment over the same distribution of peer assignments. [B28-S1, B28-S2, B28-S4; design implications]

Forbidding communication could help investigate training in an isolated setting, if the prohibition works. It would also change the workplace intervention and would not by itself eliminate competition for colleagues' time. Its result would need separate justification before being applied to a normal rollout in which employees collaborate. [B28-S2, B28-S4]
