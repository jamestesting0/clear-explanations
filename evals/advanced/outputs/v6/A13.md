**Neither reported p-value passes a Bonferroni correction for twenty outcomes.** The specified hypothetical experiment has a calculable false-positive probability, but the actual study's additional uncertainty from optional stopping cannot be reduced to an exact number from this packet.

## What can be calculated

Under the expressly hypothetical assumptions that all twenty null hypotheses are true and their final p-values are independent and uniform, each test has a 0.95 probability of avoiding a false discovery. Therefore:

\[
P(\text{at least one false discovery})
=1-P(\text{none})
=1-0.95^{20}
\approx\boxed{0.6415}.
\]

Thus about **64.15%** of repetitions of that hypothetical twenty-test experiment would produce at least one p-value below 0.05. This is not the probability that this feature has no effect. [A13-PLAN]

To control the probability of at least one false rejection across twenty tests at 0.05, Bonferroni uses the per-test threshold

\[
0.05/20=\boxed{0.0025}.
\]

| Reported outcome | Final p-value | Passes 0.0025? |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| Average saved clicks per session | 0.018 | No |
| Differently defined engagement metric | 0.041 | No |

The simple Bonferroni-adjusted value for the smallest p-value is \(\min(1,20\times0.018)=\boxed{0.36}\). These calculations use the twenty-outcome family and results in [A13-PLAN; A13-RESULTS]. Bonferroni does not require independence between outcomes, but it does require valid individual p-values under the analysis procedure.

## Why repeated looks remain a separate problem

The dashboard was inspected four times before the final analysis, with the option to stop or continue. Those looks reused accumulating participants. The hypothetical independence of the twenty final p-values does **not** make all five looks times twenty outcomes independent. The reviewer's \(1-0.95^{100}\) calculation is therefore unsupported. [A13-PLAN; A13-LOOKS; A13-DRAFT]

Choosing when to stop based on results can increase false-positive risk and can make ordinary fixed-sample p-values invalid for the procedure actually used. The missing stopping decisions, repeated results, and their joint distribution prevent an exact calculation here. In particular, multiplying 0.018 by twenty addresses the stated outcome count; it does not establish error control for the actual repeated-looking process. Collecting more observations until significance returns repeats this problem. [A13-LOOKS]

## What the result means—and does not mean

For a valid prespecified test, **p = 0.018 means that, assuming the null hypothesis and the test's other assumptions, a result at least as extreme as the observed one would occur with probability 1.8%**. It is not a 1.8% probability that the null hypothesis is true. The selection and stopping issues also limit treating this reported number as a properly calibrated confirmatory p-value. [A13-DRAFT; A13-LOOKS]

Selection can distort the estimated effect as well as its apparent certainty. Choosing the most favorable result among many outcomes can select an unusually favorable random fluctuation. The selected **+0.8 saved clicks per session** may consequently overstate the improvement that would recur in a fresh experiment; selection does not establish that the effect is zero, either. No primary outcome was prespecified. [A13-RESULTS]

The packet provides no standard error, variance, confidence interval, or sufficiently specified test from which to reconstruct one. A numerical confidence interval would be invented. Also, saved clicks are not automatically saved effort: the experiment did not measure completion time, and the meaning of a saved click matters. [A13-RESULTS]

## A concrete confirmatory follow-up

Run a fresh randomized comparison of feature versus control, assigning each user consistently to one group and accounting for repeated sessions from that user in the analysis. Before inspecting outcomes, record:

1. **Primary outcome:** average saved clicks per eligible session, with the exact counting rule, eligibility criteria, and observation window fixed in advance. This directly tests the reported claim.
2. **Stopping rule:** a fixed sample size and follow-up period, calculated from a prespecified smallest useful effect, desired power, and a variance estimate from suitable prior or separate pilot data. Conduct one final efficacy analysis; do not extend enrollment because its p-value is disappointing.
3. **Statistical decision:** use the prespecified analysis at a two-sided 0.05 level for this single primary outcome. Require an estimated benefit and a corresponding 95% confidence interval wholly above zero to claim evidence of improvement. Any additional confirmatory outcomes need their own prespecified multiplicity plan.

For the product decision, set the smallest worthwhile benefit before results arrive, then compare the estimate and interval with that threshold. Measure completion time and task success as prespecified supporting outcomes to check whether clicks represent useful effort saved. A statistically detectable change can still be too small or operationally misleading to justify rollout.
