**A doubly positive part has a 26.87% probability of being defective under the supplied population and joint detector rates.** That is higher than the 15.52% probability after A alone, but far below the memo's roughly 75%. B adds information within the A-positive group; its positive result doubles the odds there, rather than multiplying them by sixteen. [A09-BASE; A09-JOINT]

## Expected shipment counts and probabilities

Among 10,000 parts, the expected counts are `10,000 × 0.02 = 200` defective and `10,000 × 0.98 = 9,800` nondefective. Apply each joint probability to its own true-status group. [A09-BASE]

| Observed results | Expected defective | Expected nondefective | Expected total | Probability defective given those results |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A positive, regardless of B | `200 × 0.90 = 180` | `9,800 × 0.10 = 980` | 1,160 | `180/1,160 = 15.52%` |
| A positive, B positive | `200 × 0.72 = 144` | `9,800 × 0.04 = 392` | 536 | `144/536 = 26.87%` |
| A positive, B negative | `200 × 0.18 = 36` | `9,800 × 0.06 = 588` | 624 | `36/624 = 5.77%` |

These are calculations from [A09-BASE] and [A09-JOINT], rounded to two decimal places. Counts are expectations, not guaranteed realized counts in a particular shipment.

For the doubly positive group, the probability calculation can also be written directly as:

\[
P(\text{defective}\mid A+,B+)
=\frac{0.02(0.72)}{0.02(0.72)+0.98(0.04)}
=\frac{144}{144+392}
\approx26.87\%.
\]

The large nondefective population matters: even a 4% doubly positive rate among nondefective parts produces more doubly positive nondefective parts than defective ones.

## Where the memo goes wrong

The marginal detector rates are correct. Summing the relevant cells gives A a 90% detection rate among defective parts and a 10% positive rate among nondefective parts; B's corresponding rates are 80% and 5%. Those are its sensitivity and false-positive rate. [A09-JOINT]

Multiplying these marginal rates to obtain joint rates requires **conditional independence**: within each true-status group, knowing A's result must not change the probability of B's result.

| True status | Memo's multiplication | Supplied doubly positive rate |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Defective | `0.90 × 0.80 = 0.72` | `0.72` |
| Nondefective | `0.10 × 0.05 = 0.005` | `0.04` |

The product happens to match for defective parts, but fails for nondefective parts. Their actual doubly positive rate is **eight times** the memo's estimate. Using the erroneous rate would predict only `9,800 × 0.005 = 49` nondefective double positives and give `144/(144 + 49) = 74.61%`, locating the source of the memo's inflated probability. [A09-JOINT; A09-MEMO]

Independently sampling parts does not make the two detectors independent when they examine the same part. [A09-BASE; A09-JOINT]

## B's evidence after A is already positive

Once A is positive, the relevant rates for B are those **inside the A-positive subset**, separately for each true status:

\[
P(B+\mid A+,\text{defective})=\frac{0.72}{0.90}=0.80,
\]

\[
P(B+\mid A+,\text{nondefective})=\frac{0.04}{0.10}=0.40.
\]

Thus B's positive likelihood ratio in that subset—the factor by which a positive result multiplies the odds of defect—is:

\[
\mathrm{LR}_{B+\mid A+}=\frac{0.80}{0.40}=\boxed{2}.
\]

The odds after A are `180:980`, or `9:49`. Multiplying those odds by 2 gives `18:49`, corresponding to probability `18/(18 + 49) = 26.87%`. This doubles **odds**, not probability. All inputs come from [A09-JOINT].

The memo's `0.80/0.05 = 16` is B's valid marginal positive likelihood ratio when B is considered alone in this population. It is not B's additional evidence after selecting A-positive parts: within that subset, B is positive for 40% of nondefective parts, not 5%. Running B only after A changes what production observes, not the underlying joint outcomes; the complete-label validation study supplies the conditional rates needed here. [A09-BASE; A09-JOINT]

The second analyst's “literally no extra information” claim also fails. If B added no information after A, its conditional positive rates would be equal across true-status groups, producing a likelihood ratio of 1. Here they are 80% and 40%, giving a ratio of 2. Conversely, B negative has conditional rates `0.18/0.90 = 0.20` and `0.06/0.10 = 0.60`; its likelihood ratio is `1/3`, reducing defect probability to 5.77%. Dependence limits the additional evidence without eliminating it. [A09-JOINT]

## Turning probabilities into an inspection policy

The probabilities quantify risk; they do not specify how costly that risk is. A policy still needs the loss from a missed defect, the cost and consequences of inspection, the cost of inspecting a nondefective part, inspection accuracy and subsequent actions, and available inspection capacity. A double-positive-only rule would generate about 536 inspections per 10,000 parts; inspecting every A-positive part would generate about 1,160. Whether either is appropriate cannot be determined from the detector table alone.

For a simplified illustration, if inspection perfectly prevents the loss from a missed defect and costs the same for every part, its expected benefit is the part's defect probability multiplied by that loss. Comparing this benefit with inspection cost gives a threshold. Capacity constraints and imperfect inspection change that decision. The supplied packet does not provide the values needed to calculate it.

If the incoming defect rate changes while the joint conditional detector rates remain applicable, the probabilities must be recalculated using the new defect rate. If detector behavior, operating conditions, or the mix of parts within either true-status group changes, the joint conditional table also needs revalidation. That validation should preserve representative sampling within each true-status group, reliable true labels, and measurement of both detectors sufficient to estimate their joint behavior; production results observed only after A-positive selection cannot establish the missing outcomes by themselves. [A09-BASE; A09-JOINT]
