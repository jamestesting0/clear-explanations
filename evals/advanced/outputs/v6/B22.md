**The North Bell's failure to ring is plausible but unresolved; a mechanical failure is a still more specific claim that these materials do not establish.** The strongest contemporary evidence is a report that no northern answer reached one listener at the south quay. The museum should be more confident about what the diary reports than about why the bell was not heard. [B22-S1]

### Three different claims

| Claim | What would have to be true | What the packet establishes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The diarist did not hear the North Bell | No recognizable sound reached that listener | The diary directly reports this listening experience. |
| The North Bell did not ring | The bell produced no ringing during the relevant interval | Not settled by a distant listener's report. |
| Its mechanism could not ring | A physical defect prevented operation | Not settled by either non-hearing or silence alone. |

The warehouse ridge, unknown normal audibility, and possible effects of wind leave a gap between the first and second claims. A functioning bell could also remain silent because nobody operated it, leaving a separate gap between the second and third. The packet does not establish whether either alternative occurred. [B22-S1; B22-S3]

### Agreement is not necessarily independent confirmation

The diary is contemporary and tied to the storm night by its dated sequence. That makes it valuable evidence about this observer's experience, without giving the observer a view of the bell mechanism. [B22-S1]

The chronicle lists the diary among its sources, so its account of silence may depend on it. The school history's repeated unusual phrase, shared spelling error, and bibliography strongly suggest dependence on the chronicle. However, the packet does not prove the ancestry of every sentence. The appropriate conclusion is that these are **not three established independent confirmations**, rather than that every later detail is proven copied. [B22-S2]

The later books still matter. Their treatment of silence as absent leadership is evidence of how the event was represented in later historical writing and public memory. Their dependence limits their independent support for what physically happened; it does not make them worthless for studying the transmission and interpretation of the story. Nor does their date alone make them unusable. [B22-S2]

### Live explanations

**1. The bell rang, but its sound did not reach the diarist.** This fits the diary's location-specific wording. The ridge and recorded strong wind make obstructed or disrupted hearing conceivable. Modern listeners sometimes missed the reconstructed bell in strong wind, which supports the possibility of inaudibility. But different bell height and material prevent treating that test as a calibrated reconstruction of year 27, much less as proof that ringing occurred. [B22-S1; B22-S3]

**2. The bell did not ring because something prevented its operation.** The later narratives favor a reading of actual silence, although their possible dependence limits how much additional support they provide. A new rope two months later is compatible with an earlier rope problem. It does not date a failure to the storm, establish that replacement followed breakage, or identify a unique cause. [B22-S2; B22-S3]

**3. The bell was capable of ringing but was not operated.** This also fits a failure to hear it without requiring a broken mechanism. Nothing supplied establishes who attempted to ring it or whether anyone tried. The books' leadership symbolism is a later interpretation, not a contemporary operating record. [B22-S1; B22-S2]

The weather log corroborates the storm, not the bell's behavior. Festival attendance adds no evidence about its mechanism. The unsupported 97% calculation supplies no defensible confidence estimate because its assumptions about witness accuracy, dependence, and the likelihood of these records under competing explanations are ungrounded. [B22-S3; B22-S4]

### Evidence that would discriminate

A contemporaneous bell-keeper's record describing an attempted ringing and a rope breaking during the relevant interval would directly support mechanical failure. An independent nearby observer recording that the North Bell rang during that same interval would instead support the inaudibility explanation. Such records would need their timing, provenance, and independence checked; neither is supplied here.

The museum could write: “A storm-night diary reports that no answer from the North Bell reached the south quay. Later histories describe the bell as silent, but may depend on earlier accounts. Whether the bell rang, and whether a mechanical failure occurred, remain uncertain.” [B22-S1–S3]
